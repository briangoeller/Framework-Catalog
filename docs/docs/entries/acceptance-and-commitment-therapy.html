Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

Also known as: ACT

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Hayes' framework using acceptance and values clarification rather than symptom reduction.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, developed by Steven C. Hayes at the University of Nevada, Reno from the 1980s onward and presented systematically in Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson's 1999 Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: An Experiential Approach to Behavior Change, takes a fundamentally different stance from traditional CBT: rather than challenging or restructuring difficult thoughts, ACT teaches psychological flexibility through six interconnected processes — Acceptance, Cognitive Defusion, Present-Moment Awareness, Self-as-Context, Values, and Committed Action — collectively the 'hexaflex.' The aim is not symptom reduction per se but the capacity to live in accordance with chosen values even in the presence of difficult thoughts and feelings. ACT's theoretical foundation in Relational Frame Theory (Hayes and colleagues' account of human language and cognition) is more elaborated than most therapies and connects ACT to a basic-science research program. Empirical evidence supports ACT for depression, anxiety, chronic pain, substance use, and several other conditions, with effect sizes generally comparable to traditional CBT.

Core components

  • Hexaflex of psychological flexibility: Acceptance, Cognitive Defusion, Present-Moment Awareness, Self-as-Context, Values, Committed Action
  • Relational Frame Theory (RFT) basic-science foundation
  • Experiential exercises and metaphors (the bus driver, the chessboard, leaves on a stream)
  • Values clarification
  • Distinction from symptom-reduction frame
  • Functional contextualism philosophy of science

Primary use case

Treatment of depression, anxiety disorders, chronic pain, substance use disorders, OCD, and adjustment to chronic illness; growing use in workplace mental health and performance contexts; foundation for some self-help and coaching applications; integration with other contextual behavioral therapies.

Common criticisms

  • Whether ACT outperforms or matches traditional CBT remains debated — meta-analyses show comparable or modestly different effects depending on sample and condition
  • Relational Frame Theory's specific predictions are technical and some critics argue it adds complexity without clinical advantage over simpler accounts
  • metaphor-heavy approach can feel unfocused without skilled therapist execution
  • the values-based frame requires clients capable of values reflection, limiting application in some severe presentations
  • dismantling studies don't always isolate the unique contribution of acceptance vs other contextual factors
  • commercial training structure through ACBS varies in rigor.
