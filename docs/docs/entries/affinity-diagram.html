Affinity Diagram

Also known as: KJ Method

technique · management · organizing-schema

Group disparate ideas into emergent themes by intuitive sorting.

The Affinity Diagram, developed by Japanese cultural anthropologist Jiro Kawakita in the 1960s and known in Japan as the KJ Method (川喜田二郎法), is a technique for organizing large numbers of qualitative ideas, observations, or data points into emergent thematic groups through intuitive sorting. The canonical procedure: each idea is captured on a separate card or sticky note; participants silently sort cards into groups based on perceived affinity; emergent group themes are named after the sort is complete; relationships and hierarchies among themes are then identified. The method's distinctive contribution is bottom-up theme emergence — themes are discovered from the data rather than imposed from preconceived categories — making it particularly valuable in early-stage exploratory work. It is one of the seven new tools of quality (Mizuno's 1979 set complementing Ishikawa's seven basic tools) and is foundational to design thinking, contextual inquiry synthesis, and qualitative research practice.

Year / Decade

Primary sources

Kawakita, J. (1967). Hassoho [Method of Generating Ideas] (Japanese), Kawakita, J. (1991). The Original KJ Method (English) high

Core components

  • Individual idea capture on cards or notes
  • Silent sorting based on intuitive affinity
  • Emergent rather than imposed grouping
  • Group naming after sort completes
  • Relationship identification across groups
  • Hierarchy or dependency mapping
  • One of Mizuno's seven new tools of quality

Primary use case

Qualitative data synthesis in design research and contextual inquiry; brainstorming aggregation; structured discovery in exploratory phases of projects; voice-of-customer analysis; user interview synthesis; foundational practice in design thinking and service design.

Common criticisms

  • Output quality depends heavily on the breadth and quality of input ideas
  • group dynamics can produce premature closure on familiar themes
  • silent sort discipline often violated, allowing dominant voices to drive grouping
  • large-volume sorts (hundreds of items) become unwieldy
  • doesn't address frequency or importance of grouped items without additional analysis
  • tendency for emergent themes to recapitulate analyst priors
  • software adaptations (Miro, FigJam) often lose the embodied tactile dimension Kawakita emphasized.
