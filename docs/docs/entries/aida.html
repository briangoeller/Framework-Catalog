AIDA

framework · marketing · organizing-schema

Attention, Interest, Desire, Action — classic linear advertising response model.

AIDA is the foundational hierarchy-of-effects model in advertising and selling, articulating the sequence of mental states a prospect must traverse to become a customer: Attention (awareness of the product or message), Interest (engagement with what the product offers), Desire (wanting the product), Action (purchase or commitment). The framework is conventionally attributed to St. Elmo Lewis, who articulated something close to it in 1898-1899 in his work as a successful Philadelphia advertising practitioner; the specific 'AIDA' acronym emerged later through the work of E. K. Strong (The Psychology of Selling, 1925) and others. AIDA became foundational to 20th-century advertising practice and selling methodology, providing a structure for evaluating advertisements (does this ad get attention? does it create interest?...) and for organizing sales conversations. The framework's central commitment is that persuasion is a sequential cognitive process, with each stage gating the next, and that marketing communications should be designed to advance prospects through the sequence. AIDA has been substantially extended (AIDAS adding Satisfaction, AIDCA adding Conviction, AIDA(R) adding Retention/Loyalty), reframed as the Marketing Funnel, and challenged by contemporary marketing science (notably Ehrenberg-Bass empirical work) that disputes the sequential-cognitive-process foundation. The framework's pedagogical durability vastly exceeds its empirical validation.

Core components

  • Four sequential stages: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action
  • Hierarchy-of-effects assumption (each stage gates the next)
  • Application to advertisements and sales conversations
  • Foundation for many subsequent funnel and journey models
  • Subsequent extensions (AIDAS, AIDCA, AIDA(R))
  • Connection to Marketing Funnel
  • Distinction from contemporary empirical-marketing approaches that challenge sequential-cognitive assumptions
  • Substantial pedagogical role despite empirical concerns

Primary use case

Foundational framework in advertising and sales education; basis for evaluating advertisements and organizing sales conversations; pedagogical mnemonic for introducing persuasion sequence; reference framework in copywriting and direct-response marketing; foundation for Marketing Funnel and many subsequent stage-based marketing models; influence well beyond marketing into broader communication training.

Common criticisms

  • Hierarchy-of-effects assumption that persuasion proceeds sequentially through cognitive states has been substantially challenged by empirical research (Ehrenberg-Bass work, Vakratsas-Ambler 1999 'How Advertising Works' review) — much consumer behavior is habit-based, low-involvement, or driven by mental availability rather than sequential cognitive engagement
  • the framework predicts that marketing must move prospects through stages, but empirical work suggests effective marketing often works by maintaining mental and physical availability rather than through deep cognitive engagement
  • the AIDA stages are not empirically operationalized in standard ways across studies
  • popular use often deploys AIDA as decorative structure rather than analytical framework
  • cross-cultural variation in persuasion processes underweighted
  • the framework's age (125+ years) may exceed its contemporary applicability
  • commercial copywriting industry has produced AIDA as conventional structure that may not reflect actual consumer behavior.
