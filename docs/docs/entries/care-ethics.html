Care Ethics

Also known as: Ethics of Care

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Gilligan and Noddings' framework grounding ethics in relationships and responsiveness rather than abstract principle.

Care Ethics emerged in the early 1980s as a distinct ethical tradition, principally through Carol Gilligan's In a Different Voice (1982), which challenged Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory of moral development by documenting that Gilligan's interview subjects (predominantly women) reasoned about moral dilemmas through a 'different voice' emphasizing relationships, responsibility, and contextual response rather than abstract principles. Nel Noddings's Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education (1984) substantially developed care ethics as a normative tradition, distinguishing the carer (one-caring) and the cared-for in caring relations and grounding moral life in the maintenance of caring relationships. Subsequent contributors include Virginia Held, Sara Ruddick (maternal thinking), Joan Tronto (political dimensions of care), and Eva Feder Kittay (dependency and care). Care ethics is now widely recognized as a major normative tradition alongside virtue ethics, deontology, and consequentialism, with substantial influence in feminist philosophy, applied ethics (especially nursing and medical ethics), political theory of care work, and contemporary discussions of dependency. The framework's distinctive claim is that abstract impartial principle-based ethics distorts the genuine moral phenomena of relationship and responsiveness that constitute much of moral life.

Core components

  • Caring relationships as moral foundation
  • Carer (one-caring) and cared-for
  • Engrossment and motivational displacement (Noddings)
  • Distinction from impartial principle-based ethics
  • Concrete other (over generalized other)
  • Particularity and contextuality of moral situations
  • Maternal thinking as moral resource (Ruddick)
  • Political dimensions of care (Tronto's caring democracy)
  • Connection to virtue ethics (both character-focused) but distinguished by relational rather than individual focus

Primary use case

Feminist philosophy and ethics; bioethics, particularly nursing ethics and end-of-life care; moral education; political theory of care work and dependency; environmental ethics (with care extended beyond humans); applied ethics where impartial-principle approaches feel inadequate to relational moral phenomena; counterweight to abstract principle-based ethics in clinical and educational practice.

Common criticisms

  • Initial Gilligan-Kohlberg debate raised concerns about essentialist gender claims — care ethics has been substantially developed beyond the gender-binary framing of its origins, but the popular conflation persists
  • partiality concerns: care ethics seems to permit (or require) favoring one's relations, which can conflict with impartial duties of justice
  • scaling problem: how do we extend care to strangers, distant others, or future generations who are not in current caring relations
  • Sandra Bartky and others have argued some care-ethics framings risk normalizing women's traditional caregiving labor
  • tension between Noddings's emphasis on completion-in-the-cared-for and care-receiver agency
  • political-economy critique that care ethics may obscure the structural conditions that make some bodies do most of the caring work.
