Coherentism

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Knowledge justified by mutual support among beliefs, with no privileged foundation.

Coherentism is the epistemological position that the structure of justified belief is holistic rather than hierarchical: a belief is justified by its coherence with other beliefs in a comprehensive system, rather than by support from privileged 'basic' beliefs (as foundationalism holds). The position descends from German idealism (Hegel, Bradley, Bosanquet) but is substantially developed in the analytic tradition through W.V.O. Quine's 1951 'Two Dogmas of Empiricism' (with the metaphor of the web of belief, in which empirical findings affect any part of the web with adjustments propagating throughout), Wilfrid Sellars's critique of the myth of the given, and contemporary articulations by Laurence BonJour (The Structure of Empirical Knowledge, 1985), Keith Lehrer, and others. Coherence has been variously specified — logical consistency is the minimum, with stronger requirements including mutual entailment, explanatory unity, probabilistic coherence, and absence of countervailing relations. Coherentism's central challenge is the isolation objection: a coherent belief system might still be entirely disconnected from reality, with no input from the world. BonJour and others addressed this through observational requirements, but BonJour himself later abandoned coherentism for foundationalism over this concern. Reflective equilibrium is the most influential contemporary methodology grounded in coherentist principles.

Core components

  • Coherence among beliefs as justification
  • No privileged foundation or basic beliefs
  • Web of belief metaphor (Quine)
  • Holistic confirmation
  • Logical consistency as minimum coherence
  • Stronger coherence: mutual support, explanatory unity, probabilistic coherence
  • Isolation objection (coherent system might be disconnected from reality)
  • BonJour's observational requirement
  • Distinction from foundationalism on structure question
  • Connection to reflective equilibrium

Primary use case

Foundational epistemological position in analytic philosophy; framework for thinking about justification structure; foundation for reflective equilibrium methodology in ethics; influence on philosophy of science (especially Quinean naturalized epistemology); resource for thinking about scientific theory evaluation; pedagogical framework in epistemology education.

Common criticisms

  • Isolation objection: a coherent belief system might still be entirely disconnected from reality, with no input from the world — BonJour himself abandoned coherentism over this concern
  • multiple incompatible coherent systems could each be perfectly internally coherent, raising the problem of choice between them
  • specifying what coherence amounts to precisely has proven difficult
  • Bayesian and probabilistic versions of coherence have been argued to face technical problems (the Bovens-Hartmann impossibility results)
  • coherence is often a virtue without being sufficient for justification — observation, reliable processes, or other inputs seem to matter
  • Quine-style holism's prediction that any belief can be saved by adjusting other beliefs may be true in principle but practically rare
  • tension with non-doxastic states (perceptions, intuitions) that seem epistemically relevant.
