Comparative Historical Analysis

framework · history · structured-empirical

Skocpol and Tilly's methodology systematically comparing historical cases to identify causal patterns in macro-social processes.

Comparative Historical Analysis (CHA) is the systematic comparison of historical cases to identify causal patterns in macro-social processes such as revolution, state formation, economic development, democratization, and welfare-state development. The approach was substantially developed in 1960s-70s historical sociology, with Barrington Moore's Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy (1966) — comparing England, France, the United States, China, Japan, and India to identify divergent paths to modern political regimes — providing the canonical foundational text. Theda Skocpol's States and Social Revolutions (1979, comparing France, Russia, and China) consolidated the methodology, and Charles Tilly's substantial work (notably The Vendée 1964, From Mobilization to Revolution 1978, and Coercion, Capital, and European States 1990) established CHA as a foundational historical-sociological methodology. The approach uses Mill's methods of agreement and difference, more nuanced versions of case-comparison, and explicit theoretical model-testing through comparative case analysis. CHA is institutionalized through the American Political Science Association's Comparative Historical Analysis section.

Core components

  • Systematic case comparison: small-N analysis of selected historical cases
  • Mill's methods: method of agreement, method of difference, joint method
  • Causal mechanism identification: process-tracing within cases
  • Macro-historical-sociological subject matter: revolution, state formation, democratization, welfare-state development, industrialization, ethnic conflict, social-movement success
  • Theory-driven case selection: cases chosen to test specific theoretical claims
  • Critical-juncture analysis: identification of decisive moments shaping subsequent path-dependent outcomes
  • Path dependence as analytical concept (subsequent development particularly through Pierson, Mahoney, Thelen)
  • Configurational analysis (Charles Ragin's QCA / qualitative comparative analysis as methodological extension)
  • Connection to political-science methodology and historical sociology

Primary use case

Foundational methodology for historical sociology and comparative political-historical research; research framework for revolution, state formation, democratization, welfare-state development, ethnic-conflict analysis; academic reference in political-science methodology, sociology, and economic-and-political history; graduate-level methodological training in comparative-historical research; methodological foundation for historical institutionalism and path-dependence analysis.

Common criticisms

  • Comparative Historical Analysis has been substantially debated — the small-N case-comparison foundation has been challenged by statistical methodologists arguing CHA's causal claims cannot be rigorously established with the case-counts CHA typically uses, with substantial debate in political-science methodology about appropriate inferential standards (notable contributions from Gary King, Robert Keohane, Sidney Verba's Designing Social Inquiry 1994 and CHA-side responses including Mahoney, Goertz)
  • the case-selection problem is methodologically chronic — CHA studies have been argued to systematically select cases that support the theoretical claim being made (Theda Skocpol's States and Social Revolutions has been substantially critiqued on this ground by William Sewell and others)
  • the macro-historical-sociological scope produces tension with discipline-internal historiography emphasizing case-specific context — historians have argued CHA's comparative abstraction loses essential historical specificity
  • the methodology's concentration on Western and Eastern major-power cases has limited representativeness
  • integration with quantitative cross-national methods is uneven, with QCA (Ragin) attempting to bridge but remaining methodologically distinct
  • path dependence and critical juncture concepts have been argued to be post-hoc-applicable rather than predictive, with critics (William Sewell, Andrew Abbott) noting the analytical apparatus permits identifying critical junctures wherever the analyst needs them
  • the relationship to cliometrics and statistical comparative work remains methodologically contested.
