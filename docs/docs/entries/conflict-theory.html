Conflict Theory

framework · sociology · organizing-schema

Society as an arena of struggle between groups over scarce resources and power, rooted in Marx.

Conflict Theory is the macrosociological tradition treating society as fundamentally constituted by conflict between groups with opposing interests over scarce material resources, power, status, and ideological dominance — directly opposed to Structural Functionalism's emphasis on integration and stability. The tradition's foundational figure is Karl Marx (with Friedrich Engels), whose analysis of class conflict between bourgeoisie and proletariat in capitalism (Communist Manifesto 1848; Capital Volume I 1867) provided the foundational frame, with Max Weber's pluralist extension (multiple bases of conflict — class, status, party — in Economy and Society, 1922) substantially complementing Marx. American conflict theory emerged in the 1950s and 1960s through Lewis Coser (functions of social conflict, 1956), C. Wright Mills (the power elite, 1956), and Ralf Dahrendorf, with substantial subsequent development by Pierre Bourdieu (cultural capital, symbolic violence), feminist theorists (gender as conflict axis), critical race theorists, and others. The framework's analytical commitments include explaining social patterns by underlying conflicts and power asymmetries, treating institutions as instruments of dominance, and studying social change through conflict resolution. Conflict theory substantially displaced functionalism in American sociology from the 1960s onward and remains one of the principal macrosociological traditions.

Originators

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels (foundational); Max Weber (pluralist extension); Lewis Coser, C. Wright Mills, Ralf Dahrendorf (mid-20th-century American development); Pierre Bourdieu, feminist and critical-race theorists (subsequent extension) high

Year / Decade

Marx mid-19th century; Weber early 20th century; American conflict theory 1950s-60s; ongoing extensions high

Primary sources

Marx, K. & Engels, F. (1848). The Communist Manifesto, Marx, K. (1867). Capital, Volume I, Weber, M. (1922, posthumous). Economy and Society, Coser, L.A. (1956). The Functions of Social Conflict, Mills, C.W. (1956). The Power Elite high

Core components

  • Society as arena of conflict between groups
  • Conflict over resources, power, status, ideology
  • Marxist class analysis (bourgeoisie vs proletariat)
  • Weberian pluralist extension (class, status, party)
  • Connection to ideology and false consciousness
  • Bourdieu's capital extensions (economic, cultural, social, symbolic)
  • Feminist and critical-race extensions
  • Distinction from functionalism on the question of social order and change
  • Foundation for critical theory tradition

Primary use case

Macrosociological analysis particularly of inequality, power, and social change; foundation for substantial work in stratification, race, gender, and political sociology; reference framework in critical theory and political-economy traditions; basis for many activist-oriented sociological practices; foundation for class analysis in policy and labor studies.

Common criticisms

  • Reductive variants overemphasize material conflict and underweight non-conflictual sources of social order (cooperation, shared meaning, affection)
  • Marxist variants face empirical challenges from welfare-state capitalism and class structures that don't follow predicted dynamics
  • can be deployed as ideological framework rather than empirical analysis when 'conflict' is presupposed rather than demonstrated
  • multiple competing axes of conflict (class, race, gender, etc.) create coordination problems for the framework
  • distinction between productive social conflict (Coser) and pathological conflict can collapse in radical variants
  • like functionalism, can become a totalizing frame that explains everything by its preferred mechanism
  • tension between explanatory and political projects of conflict-theoretic work.
