Counterinsurgency Doctrine

Also known as: COIN

framework · military science · doctrinal-institutional

Population-centric approach to defeating insurgencies through legitimacy and protection.

Counterinsurgency Doctrine (COIN) is the population-centric military framework for defeating insurgencies through a combination of military operations, governance support, economic development, and information operations aimed at separating insurgents from the population and providing the population with security and legitimate governance. The framework's central commitment — 'the population is the prize' — distinguishes it from enemy-centric approaches focused on destroying insurgent forces. Modern COIN doctrine descends from substantial intellectual lineage including British colonial counterinsurgency (Sir Robert Thompson, Defeating Communist Insurgency 1966, drawing on Malayan Emergency experience); French counterinsurgency (David Galula, Counterinsurgency Warfare: Theory and Practice 1964, drawing on Algerian War); and substantial US Vietnam-era and post-Vietnam doctrinal work. The most influential contemporary articulation is US Army/Marine Corps FM 3-24 / MCWP 3-33.5 Counterinsurgency (2006, with David Petraeus and James Mattis as principal architects, John Nagl and others substantially involved), which substantially shaped the 2007-2008 Iraq 'Surge' strategy. FM 3-24's central principles include: legitimacy is the main objective; unity of effort across military and civilian agencies; political factors are primary; understand the environment; intelligence drives operations; security under rule of law is essential; long-term commitment is required; manage information and expectations; use measured force; learn and adapt; empower the lowest levels. The doctrine's empirical track record has been substantially contested: 2007-2008 Iraq violence reductions were substantially attributed to COIN by some analysts and to other factors (Sunni Awakening, demographic separation, Sadr ceasefire) by others; Afghanistan COIN ultimately failed to prevent Taliban return after US withdrawal in 2021. Substantial subsequent assessment has produced more measured doctrinal claims about COIN's applicability and limitations.

Primary use case

Foundation for US Army and USMC counterinsurgency doctrine; basis for substantial Iraq War (Surge) and Afghanistan War operational concepts; reference framework in counterinsurgency military education; foundation for substantial subsequent counter-violent-extremism work; integration with broader stability-operations doctrine; pedagogical foundation in counterinsurgency studies; influence on partner-nation counterinsurgency doctrine; foundation for some commercial post-conflict reconstruction consulting.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial empirical questions about COIN's effectiveness — Afghanistan War's ultimate Taliban return after US withdrawal in 2021 has prompted substantial reassessment of whether population-centric COIN's claims about defeating insurgencies through legitimacy-and-protection actually work
  • Iraq Surge violence reductions have been substantially attributed by various analysts to factors beyond COIN doctrine (Sunni Awakening, demographic separation, Sadr ceasefire)
  • Gian Gentile's substantial critique (Wrong Turn 2013) argued COIN doctrine has been substantially overhyped and that conventional military-effectiveness factors better explain US successes
  • resource-intensive nature of population-centric COIN is substantial — the troop-to-population ratios doctrine recommends are often politically and economically infeasible
  • cross-cultural application is genuinely difficult — COIN principles based on substantial cultural and political work that occupiers may not be able to perform credibly
  • tension between long-term commitment doctrine recommends and political-system tolerance for indefinite military presence
  • ethical concerns about COIN's intelligence-driven operations and aggressive interrogation practices
  • commercial popularization in 2007-2010 substantially exceeded what subsequent evidence has supported
  • integration with broader political-strategic objectives is genuinely difficult — COIN can succeed tactically while failing strategically
  • some critics argue COIN as practiced was substantially nation-building under different name, with the substantial nation-building track record (mostly limited success) applying.
