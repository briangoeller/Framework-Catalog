Five Dysfunctions of a Team

framework · management · organizing-schema

Lencioni's pyramid: absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results.

Patrick Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions of a Team, presented as a leadership fable in his 2002 book of the same name, identifies five interrelated team failure modes arranged in a pyramid where each dysfunction enables the one above: Absence of Trust (foundation: team members are unwilling to be vulnerable with each other); Fear of Conflict (built on lack of trust: artificial harmony rather than productive ideological conflict); Lack of Commitment (built on absent conflict: ambiguity and feigned buy-in rather than clarity); Avoidance of Accountability (built on weak commitment: peer pressure on behavior is inhibited); and Inattention to Results (built on accountability avoidance: team members prioritize personal status over collective outcomes). The framework's prescriptive logic is that addressing dysfunctions in order from the bottom up — starting with vulnerability-based trust — is necessary, and that team-development interventions targeting higher dysfunctions without addressing the foundation will fail.

Core components

  • Pyramid hierarchy of dysfunctions (each builds on the one below)
  • Absence of Trust (foundation)
  • Fear of Conflict
  • Lack of Commitment
  • Avoidance of Accountability
  • Inattention to Results (top)
  • Vulnerability-based trust as starting point
  • Distinction from predictive trust
  • Bottom-up intervention sequence

Primary use case

Team development and assessment; leadership coaching for team-leaders; structured team off-sites and workshops; foundation for Lencioni's consulting and assessment products; teaching tool in MBA leadership and OB courses.

Common criticisms

  • Empirical validation of the specific five dysfunctions and their pyramid relationships is limited
  • based on Lencioni's consulting experience and observational case work rather than rigorous research
  • assessment instruments are proprietary and consulting-driven
  • vulnerability-based trust as foundation may not fit all team types or cultures
  • pyramid sequencing implies addressing higher dysfunctions is futile without trust, but real teams often improve multiple dysfunctions simultaneously
  • can become a workshop framework that produces vulnerability theater without lasting team behavior change
  • risks misattributing systemic team problems to interpersonal dynamics.
