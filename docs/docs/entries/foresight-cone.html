Foresight Cone

Also known as: Cone of Plausibility

framework · management · organizing-schema

Voros's framework distinguishing possible, plausible, probable, and preferable futures across an expanding cone of uncertainty.

The Foresight Cone (also Futures Cone, Cone of Plausibility) is the schematic framework in strategic foresight and futures studies depicting the space of possible futures as expanding from the present moment, organized into concentric categories of probability, plausibility, possibility, and preposterousness. The framework was developed by Charles W. Taylor (US Army War College Strategic Studies Institute) in 1990 ('Creating Strategic Visions') and substantially elaborated by Joseph Voros (Swinburne University of Technology) in his 2003 article 'A Generic Foresight Process Framework' and subsequent work. The standard taxonomy distinguishes: Projected futures (continuation of current trends, the default future); Probable futures (likely outcomes); Plausible futures (could happen given current understanding); Possible futures (could happen in principle); Preposterous futures (deliberately transgressive provocations); and Preferable futures (normatively desired outcomes that may cut across the other categories). The cone visualizes how possibility expands with time horizon, supporting strategic-foresight exercises in identifying and exploring relevant futures.

Core components

  • Cone visualization: expanding space from present-moment apex into the future, with possibility space widening as time horizon extends
  • Standard taxonomy of future types: Projected (default continuation), Probable (likely), Plausible (could happen), Possible (might happen in principle), Preposterous (deliberately transgressive), Preferable (normatively desired)
  • Voros's elaborated taxonomy: distinguishes potential (everything beyond present), then preposterous, possible, plausible, probable, projected as nested categories
  • preferable cuts across
  • Methodological use: helps practitioners identify relevant categories of futures for specific strategic-foresight exercises (technological roadmapping, policy analysis, scenario development)
  • Distinction between exploratory futures (what might happen) and normative futures (what should happen) — the cone primarily addresses exploratory futures with preferable as normative dimension
  • Connection to other foresight techniques: scenario planning typically operates in plausible-to-possible range
  • backcasting works from preferable
  • trend extrapolation from projected-probable
  • Time-horizon variation: cone widens with longer horizons (less constraint, more possibility)
  • Critical futures studies extension: layered analysis (see Causal Layered Analysis) addresses why we treat particular futures as 'plausible' versus 'preposterous' — the cone categories themselves reflect cultural and institutional assumptions

Primary use case

Strategic-foresight exercise framework for organizing discussions of possible futures; applied principally in: corporate strategic foresight, government policy planning, military strategic planning, technology-roadmap development, futures-studies academic curriculum; Singapore Centre for Strategic Futures, UK Cabinet Office Strategic Foresight, OECD Government Foresight Community, RAND foresight practice; academic and professional reference in futures studies, strategic foresight, technology forecasting, and organizational futures literature; pedagogical reference in foresight training programs (Houston Foresight, Future Today Strategy Group, Institute for the Future); intellectual foundation for futures-literacy practice (UNESCO Futures Literacy Lab tradition); complementary to scenario planning (Wack), backcasting, Causal Layered Analysis, and other futures methods.

Common criticisms

  • The Foresight Cone has been substantially debated as framework rather than rigorous methodology — the taxonomy of future types (probable, plausible, possible, preposterous) provides useful conversational categories but the boundaries between categories are substantively undefined and depend on the analyst's judgment, raising questions about whether different practitioners applying the framework would categorize the same futures consistently
  • the cone visualization implies a quantitative structure (probability distributions widening with time) that the framework does not actually provide — without specific probability assignments, the cone is metaphor rather than analytical tool
  • the categorization of futures as 'plausible' versus 'preposterous' reflects current assumptions and biases (Inayatullah's Causal Layered Analysis substantially critiques this), with historically transformative developments (internet, smartphone, COVID-19 pandemic, large language models) having been categorized as 'preposterous' shortly before their occurrence
  • the framework's expansion-with-time visualization has been argued by complexity-theory advocates to underweight the non-monotonic structure of actual futures, where near-term possibilities can be more diverse than longer-term ones (path-dependence narrowing)
  • integration with quantitative forecasting techniques (probability distributions, Monte Carlo methods, machine-learning forecasts) is methodologically underdeveloped
  • the framework's North American and Australian intellectual lineage has been argued by Inayatullah and others to reflect particular cultural-philosophical assumptions about agency, time, and possibility that may not apply universally
  • commercial-foresight-consulting context produces varying quality in 'foresight cone' applications
  • the framework's relationship to actual strategic decision-making has been documented as uneven — foresight exercises often produce engaging conversation without substantively informing decisions in the operating-strategy rhythm.
