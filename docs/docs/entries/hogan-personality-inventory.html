Hogan Personality Inventory

Also known as: HPI

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Workplace personality assessment grounded in the Five-Factor Model.

The Hogan Personality Inventory, developed by Robert Hogan beginning in the late 1970s and published in its first edition in 1986, is a workplace-focused personality assessment grounded in socioanalytic theory and the Five-Factor Model. The HPI measures 'bright-side' personality across seven dimensions: Adjustment, Ambition, Sociability, Interpersonal Sensitivity, Prudence, Inquisitive, and Learning Approach, designed to predict normal workplace behavior. Hogan Assessments' broader suite includes the Hogan Development Survey (HDS), measuring 11 'dark-side' tendencies that emerge under stress (the only major commercial inventory built specifically to predict derailment risk), and the Motives, Values, Preferences Inventory (MVPI) measuring values and motivations. The HPI is among the most psychometrically rigorous of major commercial personality assessments, with extensive validity research supporting its use in selection, leadership development, and executive assessment contexts.

Primary sources

Hogan, R. & Hogan, J. (1995, multiple editions). Hogan Personality Inventory Manual, Hogan, R. (2007). Personality and the Fate of Organizations high

Core components

  • Seven HPI scales: Adjustment, Ambition, Sociability, Interpersonal Sensitivity, Prudence, Inquisitive, Learning Approach
  • Hogan Development Survey (HDS): 11 dark-side scales
  • MVPI (Motives, Values, Preferences Inventory): 10 scales
  • Socioanalytic theory foundation
  • Five-Factor Model grounding
  • Reputation-vs-identity distinction (others' view of you matters more than self-view for prediction)

Primary use case

Executive and managerial selection; leadership development and coaching; succession planning; team composition; one of few personality assessments specifically validated for workplace prediction; use particularly in regulated industries and senior-level selection.

Common criticisms

  • Cost limits accessibility relative to public-domain Big Five instruments
  • commercial structure with required certification limits researcher access to scoring details
  • use in selection contexts raises legal concerns in some jurisdictions
  • dark-side scale interpretation can be reductive when used for go/no-go selection rather than developmental purposes
  • cultural and translation validation work is uneven across markets
  • reputation-based item construction can confuse self-report items
  • correlations with criterion outcomes, while real, are typical of personality assessments generally (effect sizes modest).
