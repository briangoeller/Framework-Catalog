Hooked Model

Also known as: Hook Canvas

framework · marketing · organizing-schema

Eyal's trigger-action-reward-investment loop for habit-forming products.

The Hooked Model was articulated by Nir Eyal in his 2014 book Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products, presenting a four-phase cycle for designing products that create user habits. The phases: (1) Trigger — the prompt that initiates use, either external (notification, email, advertisement) or internal (emotional state, time of day, situation); (2) Action — the simplest behavior in anticipation of reward (scroll feed, tap icon, type message), with the BJ Fogg Behavior Model — B = MAT (Behavior = Motivation, Ability, Trigger) — operationalizing what enables action; (3) Variable Reward — the satisfying outcome of the action, with variability (unpredictability of timing or magnitude) being key to habit formation, drawing on Skinner's variable-reward research; types include rewards of the tribe (social validation), of the hunt (information, resources), of the self (mastery, completion); (4) Investment — the user puts something into the product (data, content, social connections, time, money) that increases the value of subsequent use and primes future triggers. The cycle repeats, building habits as the product becomes increasingly integrated into the user's life. The framework substantially shaped product design at consumer-internet companies in the 2010s, with explicit application to social media, mobile games, and consumer apps. Eyal himself has substantially engaged with subsequent ethical critique — his 2019 Indistractable addressed how users can resist hook-style design, and Eyal has distinguished 'manipulation' (causing actions users would later regret) from 'persuasion' (helping users do what they already want). The framework is craft-substantive but ethically contested, and post-2018 'Time Well Spent' / humane-tech movements have substantially critiqued the design philosophy it embodies.

Originators

Nir Eyal; intellectual antecedents in BJ Fogg's behavior model and broader habit-formation research high

Year / Decade

2014 (Hooked); 2019 (Eyal's Indistractable as response to ethical critique); ongoing development high

Primary sources

Eyal, N. (2014). Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products, Eyal, N. (2019). Indistractable (subsequent ethical engagement), Fogg, B.J. (2009). 'A Behavior Model for Persuasive Design' (intellectual antecedent), Center for Humane Technology publications (ongoing critique) high

Core components

  • Four-phase cycle: Trigger, Action, Variable Reward, Investment
  • External and internal triggers
  • Connection to BJ Fogg Behavior Model (B=MAT)
  • Variable reward drawing on Skinner research
  • Three reward types: tribe (social), hunt (resources), self (mastery)
  • Investment increases subsequent value and primes future triggers
  • Application particularly to social media, mobile games, consumer apps
  • Subsequent ethical critique and Eyal's response (Indistractable, manipulation vs persuasion distinction)
  • Connection to humane-tech and Time Well Spent movements as critique

Primary use case

Consumer-product design particularly social media, mobile gaming, consumer apps; foundation for habit-design practices in product management; reference framework in growth-marketing and product-led-growth communities; integration with behavioral-design and persuasive-technology practice; pedagogical reference in product-management and behavioral-economics education; basis for substantial commercial consulting and training business.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial ethical critique post-2018 about products designed to be habit-forming and the public-health and societal consequences of widespread habit-design (compulsive social media use, attention fragmentation, mental-health concerns particularly among adolescents)
  • Tristan Harris and the Center for Humane Technology have specifically critiqued Hooked-style design philosophy
  • concerns about manipulation vs persuasion are real — Eyal's distinction between users doing what they already want vs being manipulated into actions they would regret is contested in specific cases
  • the framework's application to addictive products (social media in particular, where mental-health harms are documented) has prompted Eyal's own subsequent work on resistance (Indistractable)
  • commercial application has produced many products that meet the criteria for harm Eyal himself articulates
  • concerns about cumulative attentional and societal effects beyond individual product-level analysis
  • tension between the framework's craft sophistication and its ethical implications
  • works principally for digital products with frequent-engagement potential
  • difficulty distinguishing 'hooks' on legitimately useful products from 'hooks' on harmful products
  • the framework continues to be taught and applied despite substantial public critique.
