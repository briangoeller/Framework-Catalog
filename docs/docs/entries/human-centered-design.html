Human-Centered Design

Also known as: HCD

framework · design · organizing-schema

Design grounded in deep understanding of user needs, behaviors, and contexts.

Human-Centered Design (HCD) is the design philosophy and family of practices grounding design decisions in deep understanding of users — their needs, behaviors, contexts, and aspirations — rather than in technical possibilities or business assumptions alone. The term and framework were substantially developed through Donald Norman's work, particularly The Design of Everyday Things (1988, originally The Psychology of Everyday Things), and through ISO 9241-210 (the international standard 'Human-centred design for interactive systems', originally ISO 13407 from 1999). HCD's central commitments include: focus on people first, technology and business second; understand actual user behavior rather than assumed user behavior; involve users throughout the design process; iterate based on user feedback; treat design as a multi-disciplinary integration of user research, technology, business, and craft. The framework is closely related to and substantially overlaps with User-Centered Design (UCD, the term Norman initially used and the principal framing in HCI traditions) and Participatory Design (the Scandinavian tradition from the 1970s emphasizing user co-design rather than user-as-research-subject). HCD has been substantially developed by IDEO into operational methodology (the Field Guide to Human-Centered Design, 2015, and the IDEO.org HCD toolkit), and is foundational to Design Thinking (separately enriched, the IDEO/Stanford d.school operationalization). The framework descends through earlier traditions including ergonomics and human factors (postwar military and aviation research), participatory design, user research traditions in HCI, and broader humanistic design philosophies. HCD is now standard practice across digital product design, service design, and increasingly in physical product design, government services, and healthcare.

Core components

  • Focus on users first
  • Deep understanding of user needs, behaviors, contexts
  • User involvement throughout design process
  • Iteration based on user feedback
  • Multi-disciplinary integration
  • ISO 9241-210 international standard
  • Connection to and overlap with User-Centered Design (UCD) and Participatory Design
  • Foundation for Design Thinking and Service Design
  • Substantial methodological development through IDEO
  • Connection to ergonomics and human factors traditions

Primary use case

Foundational design philosophy across digital product design, service design, physical product design; foundation for substantial work in HCI, UX design, service design; reference framework in design education globally; basis for many digital and physical product development processes; integration with agile and lean development practices; foundation for substantial design consulting practice; influence on government services, healthcare, and social-sector design.

Common criticisms

  • Same Design Thinking critiques apply to HCD broadly — commercial implementations vary substantially in fidelity to underlying philosophy, with much 'human-centered design' producing shallow user research and decorative empathy work rather than substantive understanding
  • tendency to focus on individual users at expense of broader systems, communities, and structural contexts (HCD's individual-user framing has been substantially critiqued by service design and systemic-design movements)
  • the 'human-centered' framing can elide whose humans are centered — design that centers some users may marginalize others (inclusive design and disability-justice critiques)
  • commercial certification and training industry has produced compliance-style HCD adoption with varying analytical fidelity
  • integration with technical and business constraints is genuinely difficult and HCD philosophy doesn't always specify how to make tradeoffs
  • cross-cultural application requires substantial cultural-knowledge investment that many implementations don't make
  • tendency to assume designers can adequately empathize with substantially different users without sustained engagement
  • the framework's emphasis on user feedback can underweight latent needs users can't articulate
  • integration with quantitative behavioral data is often weak.
