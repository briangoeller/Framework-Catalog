Interest-Based Relational Approach ⚑

Also known as: IBR

framework · negotiation · organizing-schema

Conflict-resolution approach prioritizing relationship preservation alongside interest satisfaction.

Interest-Based Relational Approach (IBR) is the negotiation and conflict-resolution framework emphasizing the joint pursuit of substantive interests and relationship preservation through structured problem-solving. The approach extends and integrates Fisher-Ury's Principled Negotiation with relationship-centered conflict-management traditions, articulated in various forms by Roger Fisher and Scott Brown's Getting Together: Building Relationships As We Negotiate (1988), and substantially developed in workplace-conflict and human-resources practice. IBR is sometimes called the Interest-Based Relational (IBR) approach in conflict-management literature and is distinguished from purely substantive interest-based negotiation by its explicit and equal weighting of relationship outcomes alongside substantive outcomes. The framework emphasizes calm communication, respectful engagement, separating the person from the problem, listening before speaking, establishing facts together, exploring options jointly, and reaching mutual agreement — operationalizing the Fisher-Ury principles in ongoing-relationship contexts.

Originators

Roger Fisher (Harvard Negotiation Project, foundational co-author of Getting Together 1988); Scott Brown (foundational co-author of Getting Together); Subsequent practitioner development across workplace-conflict-management, human-resources, organizational-development, and family-mediation traditions; intellectual antecedents in Fisher-Ury Principled Negotiation (1981), Mary Parker Follett's integrative-conflict tradition, broader interest-based mediation movement (Susskind, Folberg-Taylor); diffuse subsequent attribution — IBR has been consolidated through training and practice rather than through a single canonical text medium

Core components

  • Joint substantive-relational orientation: equal weight on substantive outcome and relationship preservation
  • Six principles often cited in IBR practice: (1) make sure good relationships are the priority
  • (2) keep people and problems separate
  • (3) pay attention to the interests being presented
  • (4) listen first, talk second
  • (5) set out the 'facts' together
  • (6) explore options together
  • Calm communication: structured techniques for managing emotional intensity in conflict, including pacing, reflection, structured turn-taking
  • Active listening: paraphrasing, reflecting, asking clarifying questions, demonstrating understanding before responding
  • Separating the person from the problem (extending Fisher-Ury principle): distinguishing substantive disagreement from relational disagreement
  • Joint fact-establishment: collaborative information-gathering rather than each party advocating their version of facts
  • Joint option exploration: brainstorming alternatives before evaluation, expanding the option set
  • Mutual agreement: outcomes both parties endorse rather than impose
  • Application contexts: workplace conflict, employee relations, family mediation, ongoing-relationship business contexts, professional disagreements
  • Distinction from purely substantive negotiation: ongoing-relationship contexts require relationship outcomes to be co-equal with substantive outcomes

Primary use case

Conflict-resolution framework for ongoing-relationship contexts where relationship preservation is co-equal with substantive outcome; applied principally in: workplace conflict resolution, employee-relations practice, human-resources mediation, family mediation and ongoing-family conflict, professional-disagreement resolution, ongoing-business-relationship contexts; training and certification in workplace mediation, human-resources practice, organizational-development consulting; academic and professional reference in conflict resolution, organizational behavior, human-resource management, and family-mediation literature; complementary to formal mediation styles (transformative, facilitative, evaluative) and broader principled-negotiation tradition; input to broader conflict-coaching practice and organizational-conflict-management infrastructure.

Common criticisms

  • IBR's status as a distinct framework versus a Principled Negotiation extension has been substantively debated — the framework draws so heavily on Fisher-Ury that some critics argue it represents repackaging rather than substantive methodological innovation, with many practitioners using 'IBR' and 'principled negotiation' interchangeably
  • the canonical-text foundation is less consolidated than for Principled Negotiation, with substantial practitioner training material varying in fidelity to Fisher-Brown's Getting Together articulation
  • the equal-weighting of substantive and relational outcomes has been argued by some negotiation scholars to underweight cases where one party's substantive interests are substantially more important than relationship preservation, producing systematic concession patterns favoring relationship-prioritizing parties
  • cross-cultural applicability is uneven — the approach's communication-discipline emphasis (calm, structured, turn-taking) reflects particular professional-Western communication norms that may not transfer cleanly to other communication contexts
  • the joint-fact-establishment principle is methodologically ambitious but practically difficult — when parties have substantively different views of facts, the process can stall or produce false agreement on compromise framings that satisfy neither
  • empirical evidence on IBR effectiveness versus alternative conflict-management approaches is dominated by case studies and self-report rather than controlled comparison
  • commercial-training context produces varying quality in IBR application, with the brand applied to conflict-management practices that lack the methodological discipline the original framework specifies
  • integration with power-imbalance contexts (employer-employee, parent-child, advantaged-disadvantaged-party situations) produces practical challenges the framework's symmetrical-orientation addresses incompletely.
