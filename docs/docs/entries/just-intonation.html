Just Intonation

Also known as: JI

framework · music theory · formal-scientific

Tuning framework based on simple integer frequency ratios derived from the harmonic series, producing pure intervals at the cost of key flexibility.

Just Intonation is the tuning framework based on simple integer frequency ratios derived from the harmonic series, producing acoustically pure intervals (perfect fifth as 3:2, major third as 5:4, minor third as 6:5, octave as 2:1) at the cost of key flexibility. In just intonation, intervals of the same nominal name (e.g., a major third) sound differently depending on which scale degree they are built from, and modulation to distant keys introduces unacceptable wolf intervals. Just intonation traces conceptually to the Pythagorean tradition (perfect fifths and octaves only as ratios) with the major third (5:4) and other simple-ratio intervals introduced through Ptolemy and codified for Renaissance polyphonic practice by Gioseffo Zarlino in Le istitutioni harmoniche (1558). Modern just-intonation advocacy traces to Harry Partch's tonality diamond (Genesis of a Music 1949) and continues through Lou Harrison, Ben Johnston, Larry Polansky, Kyle Gann, and the broader microtonal community.

Originators

Pythagoras and the Pythagorean tradition (perfect-fifth and octave ratios, ancient origin); Ptolemy (2nd century CE refinement to include 5:4 major third); Gioseffo Zarlino (Le istitutioni harmoniche 1558, Renaissance codification); Harry Partch (Genesis of a Music 1949, modern revival) high

Year / Decade

Ancient (Pythagoras, Ptolemy 2nd c. CE); 1558 (Zarlino); 1949 (Partch modern revival); ongoing microtonal tradition high

Primary sources

Zarlino, G. (1558). Le istitutioni harmoniche, Helmholtz, H. (1863). Die Lehre von den Tonempfindungen / On the Sensations of Tone, Partch, H. (1949, revised 1974). Genesis of a Music, Duffin, R.W. (2007). How Equal Temperament Ruined Harmony high

Core components

  • Frequency ratios derived from the harmonic series: 2:1 (octave), 3:2 (perfect fifth), 4:3 (perfect fourth), 5:4 (major third), 6:5 (minor third), 5:3 (major sixth), 8:5 (minor sixth)
  • Syntonic comma (81:80) — difference between Pythagorean and just major thirds
  • Wolf interval — dissonant interval produced by accumulating commas across the cycle of fifths
  • Diatonic just intonation (5-limit, using primes 2, 3, 5)
  • Higher-limit just intonation (7-limit including 7:6 septimal minor third, 11-limit, 13-limit) used by Partch and successors
  • Tonality diamond (Partch's organizing visualization)
  • Cents notation (logarithmic measure with 1200 cents per octave)

Primary use case

Tuning framework for Renaissance and early-Baroque vocal polyphony (historically informed performance practice); tuning reference for fretless and pure-tone instruments (Indian classical, barbershop quartet, certain folk traditions); compositional framework for Partch, Harrison, Johnston, La Monte Young, and the modern microtonal tradition; research framework in music-cognition and psychoacoustics; intellectual reference for tuning and temperament debates in keyboard design and historically-informed performance.

Common criticisms

  • Just intonation's keyboard implementation requires either extensive retunability or fixed-pitch instruments restricted to a single key, producing the practical limitations that motivated the historical shift to meantone and equal-tempered systems — the inability to modulate freely was the fundamental practical problem documented across centuries of tuning-system debates
  • Helmholtz's On the Sensations of Tone (1863) provided the psychoacoustic foundation for just-intonation aesthetic claims but his specific predictions about beat-rate sensitivity have been refined by subsequent psychoacoustic research
  • performance practice for Renaissance and early-Baroque vocal music remains contested between adaptive-just-intonation advocates and equal-tempered or meantone advocates, with empirical studies (Howard, Hellberg, Roederer) suggesting performers actually adopt context-sensitive intonation rather than strict adherence to any single system
  • Partch's higher-limit just intonation faces performer-training and instrument-availability barriers that limit performance access
  • the historical claim that just intonation was actually performed in Renaissance polyphony has been substantially complicated by Ross Duffin's How Equal Temperament Ruined Harmony (2007) and competing scholarship
  • cross-cultural application is uneven — Indian classical music's shruti system is sometimes equated with just intonation but the actual relationships are more complex (work by Lewis Rowell, Joep Bor).
