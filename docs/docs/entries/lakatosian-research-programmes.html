Lakatosian Research Programmes

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Lakatos' refinement of Popper: programmes have hard cores and protective belts of auxiliary hypotheses.

Imre Lakatos's Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes, presented in his 1970 paper 'Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes' (in Lakatos and Musgrave's Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge), articulates a synthesis between Popper's falsificationism and Kuhn's account of paradigms. Lakatos argued that scientific theories should be evaluated not as isolated falsifiable propositions but as research programmes — extended structures comprising a 'hard core' of central commitments treated as irrefutable by methodological decision, surrounded by a 'protective belt' of auxiliary hypotheses that absorb adjustments in light of anomalies. A research programme is 'progressive' when its successive theory adjustments predict novel facts that are subsequently corroborated, and 'degenerating' when adjustments are merely ad hoc accommodations of anomalies without novel predictive success. Programmes should be abandoned not at first failed prediction (Popper's stricter view) nor only through revolutionary replacement (Kuhn's view) but when they degenerate while a competing progressive programme is available. Lakatos's account preserves Popper's commitment to the rationality of science while accommodating the Kuhnian observation that scientists don't simply abandon theories on first refuting evidence. The framework has been substantial in philosophy of science and economics methodology (where Lakatos's work was specifically influential).

Core components

  • Research programme as unit of evaluation (not isolated theory)
  • Hard core (central commitments treated as irrefutable by methodological decision)
  • Protective belt (auxiliary hypotheses adjusted to accommodate anomalies)
  • Progressive vs degenerating problemshifts
  • Novel predictions corroborated as marker of progress
  • Competing programmes as basis for choice
  • Synthesis of Popperian falsificationism and Kuhnian paradigms
  • Distinction between sophisticated and naive falsificationism

Primary use case

Philosophy of science as bridge between Popper and Kuhn; methodology in economics (where Lakatos was directly influential through Mark Blaug and others); evaluation of long-running scientific debates and theory programmes; teaching framework in philosophy of science; resource for distinguishing legitimate theory adjustments from ad hoc rescue.

Common criticisms

  • Distinction between 'novel' predictions and 'ad hoc' adjustments is itself contested — what counts as novel can be argued retrospectively
  • 'progressive' vs 'degenerating' classifications often only become clear in hindsight, limiting the framework's prescriptive force
  • the methodology preserves the rationality of science partly by retreating to hindsight evaluation, weakening its real-time guidance
  • the hard core's irrefutability is by methodological decision rather than logical necessity, which raises questions about how the decision is justified
  • Lakatos's account doesn't well capture revolutions in pre-paradigm sciences or sciences with multiple coexisting programmes
  • integration with Bayesian and statistical accounts of evidence has been limited
  • less popular than Kuhn or Popper in contemporary philosophy of science.
