Lewin's Change Model

Also known as: Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze

framework · management · organizing-schema

Three-stage model of behavioral change at individual or organizational level.

Kurt Lewin's three-stage change model — Unfreeze (loosen current equilibrium and motivate change), Change (move to new behaviors and structures), and Refreeze (stabilize the new equilibrium so it persists) — emerged from his broader work on group dynamics and field theory at the Research Center for Group Dynamics he founded at MIT, with the canonical articulation appearing in his 1947 paper 'Frontiers in Group Dynamics' and subsequent work. The model pairs with Lewin's Force Field Analysis, which identifies the driving forces favoring change and restraining forces resisting it, with change occurring when the balance shifts. The framework is foundational to organizational development and underlies most subsequent change-management models (Kotter, ADKAR, Bridges), though scholars including Stephen Cummings and Bernard Burnes have argued that the simple 'three steps' attribution to Lewin substantially flattens his more sophisticated theoretical contribution.

Primary sources

Core components

  • Unfreeze (destabilize current equilibrium)
  • Change (transition to new state)
  • Refreeze (stabilize new equilibrium)
  • Force Field Analysis (driving and restraining forces)
  • Group dynamics and field theory underpinning
  • Action research methodology

Primary use case

Foundational framework for organizational change management; conceptual basis for subsequent change models; teaching tool in OD and change leadership courses; structuring early-stage change initiatives.

Common criticisms

  • Refreeze stage assumes a stable end-state inappropriate for continuous-change environments now common
  • oversimplifies Lewin's actual theoretical work (Cummings, Burnes critiques)
  • the three-stage attribution may be a posthumous simplification by his students rather than Lewin's own canonical formulation
  • doesn't specify mechanisms within each stage
  • treats organizations as homogeneous when subgroups may move at different paces
  • widely cited but rarely operationalized rigorously.
