Mindfulness Practice

practice · psychology · structured-empirical

Sustained non-judgmental attention to present experience.

Mindfulness Practice in its contemporary clinical form is the systematic cultivation of present-moment, non-judgmental, accepting awareness of experience — body sensations, breath, thoughts, emotions, and sensory input — as it arises and passes. The roots are in Buddhist contemplative traditions, particularly the satipaṭṭhāna (foundations of mindfulness) teachings of Theravada Buddhism and the analogous practices in Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions. The clinical translation began with Jon Kabat-Zinn's development of Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in 1979, deliberately secularized for medical settings while preserving the contemplative substance. Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT, Segal-Williams-Teasdale 2002) extended the approach for depression relapse prevention, with NHS recommendation for recurrent depression. Contemporary clinical research includes thousands of trials with mostly favorable but heterogeneous results, and MBSR/MBCT have substantial evidence for chronic pain, recurrent depression, anxiety, and stress, with smaller and more mixed evidence for many secondary applications.

Originators

Buddhist contemplative traditions (satipaṭṭhāna); Jon Kabat-Zinn (clinical translation, MBSR); Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, John Teasdale (MBCT) high

Year / Decade

Primary sources

Band notes

Originally Buddhist contemplative practice; now substantial empirical base in clinical contexts (MBSR, MBCT).

Core components

  • Present-moment awareness
  • Non-judgmental observation
  • Body scan
  • Sitting meditation with breath as primary anchor
  • Mindful movement
  • Informal mindfulness in daily activities
  • Distinction from concentration practice (focus on single object)
  • Integration with cognitive techniques in MBCT
  • Eight-week structured course format (MBSR, MBCT)

Primary use case

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) for chronic pain, stress, anxiety; Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) for depression relapse prevention; component in Dialectical Behavior Therapy and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy; workplace and organizational wellness programs; integration with sport and performance psychology; underpinning of broader contemplative neuroscience research.

Common criticisms

  • Many secondary popular and workplace applications outpace the empirical evidence base
  • meta-analyses show modest effect sizes that tend to decay at follow-up
  • secularization for clinical use has been argued to strip mindfulness of the ethical and contemplative context that gave it depth ('McMindfulness' critique)
  • decontextualized practice can produce adverse effects (anxiety, depersonalization, traumatic memory surfacing) that early enthusiasm did not adequately address — research on adverse effects has only matured in the past decade
  • commercial training varies widely in quality
  • cultural-appropriation concerns when Buddhist sources are unacknowledged
  • effectiveness may depend on substantial practice doses that many programs don't achieve.
