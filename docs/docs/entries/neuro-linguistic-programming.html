Neuro-Linguistic Programming

Also known as: NLP

framework · psychology · organizing-schema

Bandler and Grinder's framework linking neurology, language, and patterned behavior; widely used in coaching despite weak empirical support.

Neuro-Linguistic Programming was developed by Richard Bandler and John Grinder at UC Santa Cruz in the early 1970s through their attempt to model the work of effective therapists Fritz Perls (Gestalt), Virginia Satir (family therapy), and Milton Erickson (hypnotherapy). The framework's premises are that subjective experience has structure that can be modeled and replicated; that the structure is encoded in language patterns, sensory predicates, and behavioral cues; and that effective therapy and communication can be learned by modeling specific behaviors and language patterns of skilled practitioners. NLP's specific claims include: representational systems (visual, auditory, kinesthetic, olfactory, gustatory) that individuals favor and that can be detected through eye-accessing cues, predicate use, and breathing patterns; the meta-model and Milton model of language; reframing techniques; anchoring (associating internal states with specific stimuli); and submodalities (the qualities of internal representations). NLP gained substantial popular and commercial penetration in coaching, sales, and self-development, but specific empirical claims about eye-accessing cues, representational-system primacy, and modeling efficacy have been substantially disconfirmed in mainstream cognitive science, and NLP is widely classified as a pseudoscience in academic psychology.

Band notes

Cultural penetration is high; specific claims about modeling excellence and neural correlates have been disconfirmed in mainstream cognitive science.

Core components

  • Modeling excellence (the foundational claim)
  • Representational systems (VAKOG)
  • Eye-accessing cues
  • Predicates and language patterns
  • Meta-model (linguistic challenges to deletions, distortions, generalizations)
  • Milton model (linguistic patterns drawing on Erickson's hypnotic language)
  • Anchoring
  • Submodalities
  • Rapport and pacing-leading
  • Six-step reframe
  • Specific techniques (Phobia Cure, Swish Pattern)

Primary use case

Coaching, sales training, and self-development; popular workshop and certification industry; widely cited in personal-development literature and influencer culture; some clinical use, though not in evidence-based practice mainstream; commercial applications in negotiation, leadership, and persuasion training.

Common criticisms

  • Specific empirical claims have been substantially disconfirmed: eye-accessing cues do not reliably indicate cognitive processing direction
  • representational system primary preference is not consistently detectable
  • modeling specific behaviors does not transfer expertise as claimed
  • the National Research Council (1988) review found 'little if any' evidence for NLP's efficacy
  • widely classified as a pseudoscience in academic psychology and on the Skeptic's Dictionary, Quackwatch, and similar registers
  • commercial certification industry does not require evidence-based practice
  • despite cultural penetration, NLP is essentially absent from peer-reviewed evidence-based practice literature
  • ethical concerns about manipulative applications in sales and persuasion.
