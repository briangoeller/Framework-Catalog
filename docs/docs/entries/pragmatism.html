Pragmatism

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Peirce, James, Dewey: meaning and truth grounded in practical consequences and inquiry.

Pragmatism is the principal indigenous American philosophical tradition, founded by Charles Sanders Peirce in the 1870s through the Metaphysical Club in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and substantially developed by William James (Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking, 1907), John Dewey (across many works including Democracy and Education 1916, Experience and Nature 1925), and George Herbert Mead. The tradition's defining commitments include the pragmatic maxim (Peirce: 'Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object'); a fallibilist epistemology that treats inquiry as ongoing self-correction rather than approach to fixed truth; a process-oriented metaphysics in which reality is constituted through interaction; and a democratic political philosophy treating intelligence as socially distributed and inquiry as a community practice. The tradition was eclipsed by analytic philosophy in mid-20th-century academic philosophy but underwent substantial revival from the 1970s onward, principally through Richard Rorty (neopragmatism), Hilary Putnam, and Cheryl Misak. Pragmatism has had outsized influence beyond philosophy in education (Dewey), social science methodology (action research), legal theory (Holmes, Posner), and political theory.

Year / Decade

1870s (Peirce's Metaphysical Club); 1907 (James's Pragmatism); 1979 (Rorty's Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature, neopragmatist revival) high

Primary sources

Peirce, C.S. (1878). 'How to Make Our Ideas Clear', Popular Science Monthly, James, W. (1907). Pragmatism, Dewey, J. (1925). Experience and Nature, Rorty, R. (1979). Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature high

Core components

  • Pragmatic maxim (meaning grounded in practical consequences)
  • Fallibilism (knowledge as ongoing self-correction)
  • Inquiry as community practice
  • Truth as what works in inquiry (with substantial variation across pragmatists)
  • Anti-foundationalism
  • Process metaphysics
  • Connection between thought and action
  • Dewey's instrumentalism
  • Democracy as inquiry
  • Distinction from positivism (broader conception of what 'works')

Primary use case

Foundational American philosophical tradition; basis for Dewey's progressive education theory; influence on legal realism (Holmes); methodological foundation for action research and design research; influence on contemporary philosophy of science and language; resource for contemporary debates about truth, objectivity, and democratic inquiry; basis for some contemporary political theories of inquiry.

Common criticisms

  • Critics including Bertrand Russell argued early pragmatism conflated truth with utility, with disturbing implications for what 'works' in totalitarian contexts
  • James's particular formulations were sometimes loose enough to invite caricature ('the cash-value of truth')
  • Rorty's later neopragmatism abandoned much of the classical tradition's substantive metaphysics, leading some classical pragmatists to argue Rorty wasn't really pragmatist
  • pragmatism's anti-foundationalism makes specific claims hard to evaluate against
  • tradition's diversity (classical, neo-, analytical) creates which-pragmatism-when ambiguity
  • some critics argue pragmatism sacrifices realism and objectivity in ways that don't survive close examination.
