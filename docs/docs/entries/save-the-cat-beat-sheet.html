Save the Cat Beat Sheet

framework · narrative studies · organizing-schema

Snyder's fifteen-beat screenplay structure widely used in Hollywood.

The Save the Cat Beat Sheet is the fifteen-beat screenplay structural framework articulated by Hollywood screenwriter Blake Snyder in his 2005 Save the Cat! The Last Book on Screenwriting You'll Ever Need (with subsequent Save the Cat Goes to the Movies 2007 and Save the Cat Strikes Back 2009). Snyder's framework operationalized three-act structure (separately enriched) into specific beats with target page numbers in a standard 110-page screenplay: (1) Opening Image (page 1, sets tone); (2) Theme Stated (page 5); (3) Setup (pages 1-10, introduces protagonist's world); (4) Catalyst (page 12, inciting incident); (5) Debate (pages 12-25, protagonist's reluctance); (6) Break Into Two (page 25, commitment to journey); (7) B Story (page 30, secondary plot, often love interest); (8) Fun and Games (pages 30-55, 'promise of the premise' delivers genre expectations); (9) Midpoint (page 55, false victory or false defeat); (10) Bad Guys Close In (pages 55-75); (11) All Is Lost (page 75, lowest point); (12) Dark Night of the Soul (pages 75-85); (13) Break Into Three (page 85, solution emerges); (14) Finale (pages 85-110, climactic action); (15) Final Image (page 110, mirror of opening). The 'save the cat' title refers to Snyder's principle of giving the protagonist a likeable early action (literally saving a cat) to win audience sympathy. The framework has been enormously influential in Hollywood screenwriting from the mid-2000s onward — substantial proportion of contemporary mainstream films can be analyzed through Snyder's beats, with substantial commercial training and software (Save the Cat software, online courses, certification programs) building on Snyder's methodology. The framework has also drawn substantial criticism for producing formulaic mainstream films — substantial Hollywood discourse blames 'Save the Cat formula' for the perceived sameness of contemporary mainstream cinema, with substantial published criticism arguing Snyder's beats homogenize creative work that should be distinctive.

Core components

  • Fifteen beats with specific target page numbers in 110-page screenplay
  • 'Save the cat' principle (likable early action for protagonist)
  • Connection to three-act structure with finer-grained beat specification
  • Beats include: Opening Image, Theme Stated, Setup, Catalyst, Debate, Break Into Two, B Story, Fun and Games, Midpoint, Bad Guys Close In, All Is Lost, Dark Night of the Soul, Break Into Three, Finale, Final Image
  • Genre-specific guidance (Snyder's ten genre categories)
  • Substantial Hollywood adoption from mid-2000s
  • Commercial training and software ecosystem
  • Foundation in three-act-structure tradition with substantial Hollywood operationalization

Primary use case

Foundational structural framework in contemporary Hollywood screenwriting; basis for substantial screenplay development since mid-2000s; reference framework in contemporary screenwriting education; foundation for substantial commercial screenwriting consulting and training; integration with three-act-structure tradition; pedagogical foundation in commercial screenwriting curricula; influence on television writing, novel-writing, and other narrative forms; foundation for substantial Save the Cat franchise (software, courses, certification programs).

Common criticisms

  • Substantial Hollywood criticism that 'Save the Cat formula' produces formulaic, predictable mainstream cinema — many critics argue contemporary Hollywood films feel structurally similar due to Snyder's beats being applied as template rather than diagnostic tool
  • the rigid page-count specifications produce mechanical structure that crowds out distinctive creative choice
  • commercial Save the Cat industry has substantial financial stake in formula adoption that may shape what gets developed and produced
  • tendency for Snyder's beats to be applied as checklist rather than as analytical framework, producing screenplays that hit beats without substantive narrative quality
  • Save the Cat works better for action, thriller, and romantic-comedy genres (Snyder's areas of substantial commercial success) than for character-driven drama, art-house cinema, or experimental work
  • the framework substantially privileges plot-event structure over character development, theme, atmosphere, and other dramatic elements
  • cross-cultural application of American-Hollywood framings has uneven results
  • Snyder's confident assertions ('the last book on screenwriting you'll ever need') have produced uncritical adoption that subsequent reflection has substantially complicated
  • integration with novelistic and theatrical traditions is incomplete
  • tendency for screenwriting students trained primarily in Save the Cat to produce screenplays that read as formulaic rather than distinctively creative
  • the framework's commercial dominance in screenwriting education has marginalized substantively different structural frameworks (four-act, eight-sequence, episodic structures).
