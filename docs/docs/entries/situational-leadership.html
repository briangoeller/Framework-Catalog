Situational Leadership

framework · management · organizing-schema

Hersey-Blanchard model matching leadership style to follower readiness.

Situational Leadership, developed by Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard at Ohio University in the late 1960s and presented in Management of Organizational Behavior (1969), holds that effective leadership style varies with the readiness or development level of the follower for the specific task at hand. The framework defines four leadership styles along two dimensions — task behavior (directive) and relationship behavior (supportive) — yielding S1 Directing (high task, low relationship), S2 Coaching (high task, high relationship), S3 Supporting (low task, high relationship), and S4 Delegating (low task, low relationship). Follower readiness is correspondingly mapped: R1 (low ability, low willingness) calls for S1; R2 (low ability, high willingness) for S2; R3 (high ability, low willingness) for S3; R4 (high ability, high willingness) for S4. Hersey and Blanchard split in 1985, leading to Hersey's 'Situational Leadership' brand and Blanchard's 'Situational Leadership II' (SLII), which renamed the dimensions and styles slightly while preserving the contingency logic.

Originators

Paul Hersey; Ken Blanchard high

Year / Decade

1969 (Management of Organizational Behavior); 1985 (Hersey-Blanchard split, SLII) high

Primary sources

Hersey, P. & Blanchard, K.H. (1969, multiple editions). Management of Organizational Behavior, Blanchard, K., Zigarmi, P. & Zigarmi, D. (1985). Leadership and the One Minute Manager high

Core components

  • Two style dimensions: Task behavior (directive)
  • Relationship behavior (supportive)
  • Four styles: S1 Directing, S2 Coaching, S3 Supporting, S4 Delegating
  • Four follower readiness levels: R1-R4
  • Style matched to follower readiness for specific task
  • Distinction between SL (Hersey) and SLII (Blanchard)

Primary use case

Leadership development and training; manager coaching on adapting style to direct reports' development levels; teaching tool in management education; corporate leadership programs.

Common criticisms

  • Empirical validation of the specific contingency predictions is mixed and inconsistent across studies
  • readiness assessment is subjective and easily biased by manager preferences
  • treats follower development as task-specific but in practice managers tend to fix on global style rather than adapting per task
  • commercial certification through Center for Leadership Studies (Hersey) and Ken Blanchard Companies limits independent assessment
  • differences between SL and SLII brands create market confusion
  • over-simplifies the relational and political dimensions of management
  • doesn't address situations where the leader's appropriate style is constrained by structure, peer norms, or culture.
