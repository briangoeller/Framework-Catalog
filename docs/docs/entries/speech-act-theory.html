Speech Act Theory

framework · linguistics · structured-empirical

Austin and Searle's framework: utterances perform actions (locutionary, illocutionary, perlocutionary).

Speech Act Theory is the pragmatic framework analyzing utterances as performances of actions rather than merely as descriptions of states of affairs, articulated by J.L. Austin in his foundational 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard (published posthumously as How to Do Things with Words 1962) and substantially developed by John Searle (Speech Acts 1969). Austin's central observation: many utterances ('I promise to call you,' 'I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth,' 'I now pronounce you married') don't describe pre-existing states but rather perform actions through their utterance — what Austin initially called 'performatives' as opposed to 'constatives,' before his subsequent analysis showing all utterances have action-performing dimensions. Austin's three-level analysis: (1) Locutionary act — the utterance itself with its conventional meaning (what is said); (2) Illocutionary act — the action performed in saying it (assertion, promise, request, command, naming, etc. — the illocutionary force); (3) Perlocutionary act — the effects produced by saying it (persuading, convincing, scaring, amusing the hearer). Searle substantially systematized speech act theory with: typology of illocutionary acts (assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, declarations); felicity conditions (preparatory, sincerity, propositional content, essential conditions specifying what makes a particular speech act successful); indirect speech acts (saying one thing while meaning another, e.g., 'Can you pass the salt?' as request rather than question). The framework substantially shaped pragmatics, philosophy of language, legal theory (J. L. Austin's analysis influenced legal-philosophical work on performatives in contracts and constitutive utterances), and broader analytical philosophy. Recent applications include AI/NLP work on dialogue systems, speech act recognition, and formal semantics-pragmatics interfaces.

Core components

  • Three levels: locutionary (utterance with meaning), illocutionary (action performed in saying), perlocutionary (effects produced)
  • Performatives vs constatives (early Austin)
  • Searle's typology: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives, declarations
  • Felicity conditions for successful speech acts
  • Indirect speech acts
  • Application to philosophy of language, legal theory, AI dialogue systems
  • Foundation for pragmatics
  • Distinction from purely truth-conditional semantics
  • Substantial influence on analytical philosophy

Primary use case

Foundational framework in pragmatics and philosophy of language; basis for substantial work on dialogue analysis, conversational AI, legal philosophy of performatives; reference framework in pragmatics and philosophy of language education; foundation for substantial linguistic-philosophical analyses of communication; integration with broader cognitive science of language; pedagogical foundation in linguistic pragmatics and analytical philosophy curricula; influence on speech-recognition and dialogue-system design in AI; foundation for some legal-philosophical work on constitutive utterances.

Common criticisms

  • Austin and Searle's typology of illocutionary acts has been substantially debated — different scholars classify acts differently with no clear empirical basis for choosing among classifications
  • felicity conditions are conceptually clean but operationally varied — what counts as the 'right' preparatory or sincerity conditions for any given speech act involves substantial interpretive choice
  • cross-cultural variation in speech-act conventions is substantial — different cultures perform requests, promises, apologies through substantially different conventional forms
  • integration with broader pragmatic theories (Cooperative Principle, Relevance Theory, separately enriched) has produced productive debate but also conceptual proliferation
  • tendency in some applications to use speech act terminology decoratively without substantive analytical work
  • the framework's emphasis on individual utterances can underweight broader discourse-level phenomena that contemporary discourse analysis addresses
  • AI dialogue systems applications have substantially extended speech act theory in directions Austin and Searle didn't develop, with varying analytical fidelity to original framework
  • commercial dialogue-system marketing sometimes overstates what speech act recognition actually accomplishes
  • Searle's later work on social ontology (Construction of Social Reality 1995) has produced substantial debate about how performative speech acts create institutional facts.
