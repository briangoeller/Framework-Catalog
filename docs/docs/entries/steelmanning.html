Steelmanning ⚑

technique · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Construct the strongest possible version of an opposing argument before engaging it.

Steelmanning is the rhetorical and intellectual practice of constructing the strongest possible version of an opposing argument before engaging it — the deliberate inverse of strawmanning (engaging with a weakened or distorted version of an opposing position). The term emerged in rationalist communities (particularly LessWrong, where it was popularized by Eliezer Yudkowsky and others) in the late 2000s and early 2010s, building on much older intellectual traditions of charitable interpretation including the Talmudic principle of debating the strongest version of opposing arguments, John Stuart Mill's emphasis in On Liberty (1859) on engaging the best version of competing views ('he who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that'), and the principle of charity in philosophy (Davidson, Quine — interpret others' utterances so as to maximize their truth or rationality). The practice is closely related to Sturgeon's Law (engage with the best examples), but distinct: Sturgeon addresses sampling across a field, while steelmanning addresses argument-by-argument engagement. Effective steelmanning requires genuine effort to understand and improve the opposing position — even articulating it more compellingly than its actual proponents have — before engaging. The practice has obvious epistemic and ethical virtues but also limits: when the strongest version of an argument is genuinely much stronger than what proponents actually argue, steelmanning can become an exercise in straw-manning the proponents while engaging an artifact of one's own construction.

Core components

  • Construct strongest version of opposing argument
  • Inverse of strawmanning
  • Connection to principle of charity (Davidson, Quine)
  • Mill's argument in On Liberty for engaging best opposing views
  • Distinction from Sturgeon's Law (argument-level vs field-level)
  • Limits when steelman differs substantially from actual proponents' position
  • Genuine effort to improve opposing position
  • Pre-engagement step in serious argument

Primary use case

Intellectual practice in serious argument; debiasing tool against motivated reasoning; rationalist-community methodology; teaching tool for substantive engagement; complement to strong-style philosophy and analytic argument; basis for many practices around productive disagreement; reference in contemporary online intellectual culture.

Common criticisms

  • When the strongest version of an argument is substantially stronger than what proponents actually argue, steelmanning can become engagement with an artifact rather than with the actual position — and proponents may legitimately object that the steelman is not their argument
  • effort required for genuine steelmanning is high and most casual deployment of the term is closer to weak charity
  • ironymanning (constructing a position so reasonable it resembles the opponent's actual position less than a charitable representation) is a known failure mode
  • in some cases the actual weakness of an argument is the point — steelmanning weak arguments may misrepresent the field of opposition
  • can become a rhetorical move signaling intellectual virtue without actually engaging with what the opponent is saying
  • tension with respecting opponents' actual stated views
  • the term itself has been argued to be needlessly martial.
