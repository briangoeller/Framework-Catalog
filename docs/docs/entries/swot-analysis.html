SWOT Analysis ⚑

tool · management · organizing-schema

Surfaces strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats for a venture or decision.

SWOT Analysis is a strategic planning technique that organizes assessment of a venture, decision, or organization into four quadrants: internal Strengths and Weaknesses, and external Opportunities and Threats. Its origin is contested but is generally attributed to a Stanford Research Institute project in the 1960s led by Albert Humphrey, with the original framework called SOFT (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat) before evolving to SWOT through subsequent practitioner use. Kenneth Andrews's articulation of internal capabilities matched against external environment in his contribution to the 1965 Harvard Business Policy textbook substantially shaped the analytical structure, and George Steiner and Heinz Weihrich's TOWS matrix (1982) extended SWOT into a strategy-generation tool by crossing internal and external factors to surface SO, ST, WO, and WT strategies. SWOT's accessibility has made it ubiquitous in strategic planning, though academic strategy scholars have long critiqued it as substituting brainstorming for analysis.

Originators

Albert Humphrey and Stanford Research Institute team (typically credited, though attribution is contested); Kenneth Andrews (analytical structure); Heinz Weihrich (TOWS matrix extension) medium

Year / Decade

1960s (SRI development); 1965 (Andrews framing); 1982 (Weihrich TOWS) medium

Primary sources

Andrews, K.R. (1971). The Concept of Corporate Strategy, Weihrich, H. (1982). 'The TOWS Matrix — A Tool for Situational Analysis', Long Range Planning medium

Core components

  • Strengths (internal positive)
  • Weaknesses (internal negative)
  • Opportunities (external positive)
  • Threats (external negative)
  • 2x2 grid (internal-external x positive-negative)
  • TOWS extension matching internal to external for strategy options

Primary use case

Strategic planning workshops; structured discussion-starter for strategy formulation; teaching tool in introductory strategy and entrepreneurship courses; foundational analytical artifact in business plans.

Common criticisms

  • Generates lists rather than analysis — items in each quadrant are typically not prioritized, weighted, or causally connected
  • same factor can plausibly appear in multiple quadrants depending on framing
  • substitutes brainstorming for empirical investigation of competitive position
  • does not generate strategy directly without TOWS or similar extension
  • static snapshot without dynamics
  • can confirm rather than challenge existing strategic priors
  • widely used as a perfunctory ritual rather than a serious analysis.
