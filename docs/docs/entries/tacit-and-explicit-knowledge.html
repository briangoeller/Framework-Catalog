Tacit and Explicit Knowledge

framework · knowledge management · organizing-schema

Polanyi's distinction between articulable knowledge and the embodied 'we know more than we can tell.'

The Tacit and Explicit Knowledge distinction was articulated by Hungarian-British polymath Michael Polanyi (chemist, philosopher, economist) in his foundational Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy (1958) and substantially developed in The Tacit Dimension (1966). Polanyi's central insight, captured in his famous phrase 'we know more than we can tell,' is that substantial human knowledge is tacit — embodied, intuitive, experiential — and resists complete articulation in propositional form. Tacit knowledge includes: skills (riding a bicycle, recognizing faces, judging clinical situations) that practitioners can perform but cannot fully describe in rules; perceptual abilities (recognizing patterns, judging quality, sensing social dynamics); understanding gained through substantial experience and immersion that exceeds what manuals or training documents can convey. Explicit knowledge by contrast is articulable in propositional form — facts, rules, formulas, documented procedures. Polanyi argued that all explicit knowledge rests on substantial tacit foundation — even mathematical reasoning depends on tacit understanding of what counts as a valid proof, recognition of relevant similarities, and skill in problem-solving that cannot be fully formalized. The framework has had enormous influence beyond Polanyi's original philosophical context. The substantial 1990s knowledge-management movement substantially adopted the distinction, particularly through Ikujiro Nonaka and Hirotaka Takeuchi's The Knowledge-Creating Company (1995) — their substantial SECI model (Socialization, Externalization, Combination, Internalization) substantially proposed that organizational knowledge creation involves continuous conversion between tacit and explicit forms. Critics including Stephen Gourlay (2006 'Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory') have argued that Nonaka-Takeuchi's interpretation substantively misreads Polanyi — Polanyi treated tacit and explicit as integrated dimensions of knowledge rather than as separable types that can be 'converted.' Despite these debates, the tacit-explicit distinction remains foundational to knowledge management and broader cognitive science.

Core components

  • Tacit knowledge as embodied, experiential, resistant to articulation
  • Explicit knowledge as propositional, articulable, documented
  • 'We know more than we can tell' (Polanyi's foundational claim)
  • All explicit knowledge resting on tacit foundation
  • Substantial Nonaka-Takeuchi SECI model extension (1995)
  • Connection to skills, perception, experiential learning
  • Distinction from purely propositional accounts of knowledge
  • Foundation for substantial knowledge-management practice
  • Influence beyond knowledge management into cognitive science, philosophy, education
  • Substantial debate about Nonaka-Takeuchi's reading of Polanyi

Primary use case

Foundational distinction in knowledge management and cognitive science; basis for substantial work on organizational knowledge transfer, expert systems, AI knowledge representation; reference framework in knowledge-management education globally; foundation for substantial Nonaka-Takeuchi SECI model and broader Japanese knowledge-management tradition; integration with broader philosophy of knowledge and epistemology; pedagogical foundation in knowledge-management curricula; influence on apprenticeship, mentoring, and professional-development design; foundation for substantial commercial knowledge-management consulting practice; basis for many AI/ML approaches that recognize limits of explicit knowledge representation.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial debate about whether Nonaka-Takeuchi's interpretation substantively misreads Polanyi — Stephen Gourlay's 2006 'Conceptualizing Knowledge Creation: A Critique of Nonaka's Theory' argued Polanyi treated tacit and explicit as integrated dimensions rather than as separable types that can be 'converted'
  • the SECI model's substantial commercial popularization has produced widespread adoption with varying analytical fidelity to Polanyi's substantive framework
  • commercial knowledge-management vendors have produced substantial 'tacit knowledge capture' tools that may oversimplify what tacit knowledge actually involves — much tacit knowledge cannot be captured in databases
  • cross-cultural application of European-American framework has uneven results — different cultures have substantially different relationships with embodied vs explicit knowledge
  • integration with AI/ML systems is genuinely difficult — large language models have demonstrated substantial capabilities that complicate strict tacit-explicit distinction (LLMs trained on text alone exhibit some abilities previously thought to require tacit understanding)
  • tendency for 'tacit knowledge' to be invoked decoratively when explicit-knowledge approaches are deemed insufficient
  • the framework's philosophical depth (Polanyi's substantial epistemological work) is often substantially flattened in commercial knowledge-management applications
  • integration with implicit-cognition research (priming, statistical learning, embodied cognition) is productive but incomplete in many applications
  • some critics argue the binary tacit-explicit distinction obscures continuous variation in articulability
  • SECI model's continuous-conversion claim has been substantially questioned for whether tacit-to-explicit conversion is genuinely possible or whether 'conversion' actually produces different types of knowledge.
