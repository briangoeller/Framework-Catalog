Theory of Communicative Action

framework · sociology · organizing-schema

Habermas' framework distinguishing instrumental from communicative rationality.

Jürgen Habermas's Theory of Communicative Action was articulated in his two-volume Theorie des kommunikativen Handelns (1981, English translation 1984-1987) and is the principal second-generation Frankfurt-School reformulation of Critical Theory. Habermas distinguished four types of action: teleological/instrumental (oriented to success in achieving goals through control of objects); strategic (instrumental action oriented to success in influencing other actors as objects); norm-regulative (oriented to compliance with shared norms); and communicative (oriented to mutual understanding through unconstrained reasoned argumentation). Communicative rationality — embedded in the implicit validity claims of speech acts (truth, normative rightness, sincerity, comprehensibility) — provides the foundation for democratic legitimacy and rational social order, and is normatively prior to instrumental rationality. The 'system' (economy and state, organized by money and power) and the 'lifeworld' (the everyday domain of communicative action and shared meaning) coexist in modern societies, but the 'colonization of the lifeworld' by system-imperatives represents pathologies of modernity. Habermas's framework substantially repositioned Critical Theory away from the first generation's pessimism (Dialectic of Enlightenment) and toward a normative grounding in the implicit rationality of communication. The framework has had enormous influence in social theory, political theory (deliberative democracy), legal theory (procedural justification), and ethics (discourse ethics). Critics including Foucault, Lyotard, and feminists have argued that the appeal to ideal communication abstracts from power and difference in problematic ways.

Core components

  • Four action types: teleological/instrumental, strategic, norm-regulative, communicative
  • Communicative rationality grounded in validity claims (truth, rightness, sincerity, comprehensibility)
  • System (money and power) vs lifeworld (communicative action)
  • Colonization of the lifeworld
  • Discourse ethics (Diskursethik)
  • Ideal speech situation
  • Foundation for deliberative democracy
  • Distinction from first-generation Frankfurt School pessimism
  • Connection to broader project of grounding critical theory in rational communication

Primary use case

Social and political theory; foundation for deliberative-democracy theory and practice; basis for substantial work in democratic theory, public-sphere analysis, and political legitimacy; influence on legal theory (procedural justification of democratic legitimacy); foundation for discourse ethics; reference framework in critical theory's second generation; influence on practical applications including stakeholder dialogue, deliberative polling, and public engagement design.

Common criticisms

  • Ideal speech situation and unconstrained communication abstractions have been argued to abstract from real power asymmetries that make ideal conditions unattainable
  • Foucault's critique that the appeal to communication-as-foundation underweights how power-knowledge produces what counts as rational
  • feminist critiques (Iris Marion Young, Seyla Benhabib) that the rationality criteria embed culturally specific norms about argument that exclude other communicative modes (story, testimony, rhetoric)
  • postmodern critiques (Lyotard) of grand-narrative grounding in rationality
  • substantial complexity of the framework limits accessibility
  • tension between Habermas's substantive engagement with empirical social science and his normative-philosophical grounding
  • the practical operationalization of deliberative democracy in actual political institutions has been mixed
  • cross-cultural application of European-grounded framing.
