Transtheoretical Model

Also known as: Stages of Change

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Prochaska and DiClemente's stages: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance.

The Transtheoretical Model was developed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente at the University of Rhode Island through comparative analysis of psychotherapy systems in the late 1970s, presented in The Transtheoretical Approach (1984), and substantially extended to behavior-change contexts in the 1990s. The framework proposes that change unfolds through five (later six) stages: Precontemplation (no intention to change in the foreseeable future, often unaware of the problem); Contemplation (aware of the problem, considering change but not committed); Preparation (intending to take action soon, often with small initial steps); Action (overt behavior change); Maintenance (sustained change for at least six months); and Termination (where the new behavior is fully integrated and relapse risk is minimal). The model also articulates ten processes of change, decisional balance (pros and cons of changing), and self-efficacy as components, with stage-matched interventions theorized to be more effective than stage-mismatched ones. TTM has been substantially applied to smoking cessation (its origin domain) and extended to many other health behaviors.

Core components

  • Five (or six) stages of change: Precontemplation, Contemplation, Preparation, Action, Maintenance, (Termination)
  • Ten processes of change (consciousness raising, dramatic relief, self-reevaluation, environmental reevaluation, self-liberation, social liberation, counterconditioning, helping relationships, reinforcement management, stimulus control)
  • Decisional balance (pros and cons)
  • Self-efficacy
  • Stage-matched interventions hypothesis

Primary use case

Smoking cessation programs (origin domain); health behavior change broadly (diet, exercise, alcohol, drug use); foundation for stage-matched intervention design; widely cited in health communication and motivational interviewing training; basis for many corporate wellness program designs.

Common criticisms

  • West's 2005 'Time for a change: putting the Transtheoretical (Stages of Change) Model to rest' argued the discrete stages are arbitrary and not empirically supported as distinct categories — proposed stage boundaries are based on time intervals rather than psychological discontinuities
  • stage-matched intervention superiority claim has not been consistently demonstrated in meta-analyses
  • cyclical/spiral structure (relapse to earlier stages) is more rule than exception, complicating stage-matched application
  • classification of individuals into stages is methodologically unstable
  • popular adoption substantially overclaims what evidence supports
  • doesn't well address structural barriers and social determinants of behavior change.
