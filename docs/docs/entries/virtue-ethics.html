Virtue Ethics

Also known as: Aretaic Ethics

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Ethics centered on character traits and human flourishing rather than rules or consequences.

Virtue Ethics centers moral inquiry on character — on what kind of person one should become — rather than on action-evaluating principles (Deontology) or outcome-evaluating calculations (Consequentialism). The tradition's foundational text is Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, which articulates eudaimonia (human flourishing or the good life) as the proper end of human action and the virtues (excellences of character — courage, temperance, justice, practical wisdom or phronesis, and others) as the stable dispositions through which a flourishing life is constituted. After dominating Western moral philosophy through medieval Christianity (Aquinas's Christianized Aristotelianism), virtue ethics receded under Enlightenment-era enthusiasm for principle-based ethics, and was substantially revived in the 20th century by Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 'Modern Moral Philosophy', Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue (1981), Philippa Foot's Natural Goodness (2001), and Rosalind Hursthouse's On Virtue Ethics (1999). The framework remains a major contemporary normative tradition alongside Deontology and Consequentialism, with applications including professional ethics, virtue epistemology, and political theory.

Originators

Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics); Plato as antecedent; Thomas Aquinas (Christian synthesis); 20th-century revival by Elizabeth Anscombe, Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre, Rosalind Hursthouse, Martha Nussbaum high

Year / Decade

4th century BCE (Aristotle); 1958 (Anscombe revival); 1981 (MacIntyre After Virtue) high

Primary sources

Aristotle. Nicomachean Ethics, Anscombe, G.E.M. (1958). 'Modern Moral Philosophy', Philosophy, MacIntyre, A. (1981). After Virtue, Hursthouse, R. (1999). On Virtue Ethics high

Core components

  • Eudaimonia (human flourishing) as ethical end
  • Virtues as stable character dispositions
  • Phronesis (practical wisdom) as integrating capacity
  • Doctrine of the mean (virtue as between excess and deficiency)
  • Distinction from rule-based and outcome-based ethics
  • Habituation as moral education
  • Tradition-bearing communities (MacIntyre)
  • Care ethics as related contemporary tradition

Primary use case

Normative ethical theory in academic philosophy; professional ethics (medicine, law, business); foundation for virtue epistemology; influence on positive psychology and character-strengths research; applied ethics including bioethics and business ethics; political philosophy (especially MacIntyre's communitarian critique).

Common criticisms

  • Action-guidance objection: virtue ethics tells you what kind of person to be but not what to do in specific cases
  • cultural variability of virtue lists raises relativist concerns
  • Aristotle's specific virtue list reflects Greek aristocratic culture and excludes major moral concerns (women, slaves, animals)
  • 'doctrine of the mean' resists precise specification
  • situationist critique (Doris, Harman) from social psychology argues that empirical evidence on cross-situational consistency of personality traits undercuts virtue ethics' character-trait foundations — though virtue ethicists have responded substantively
  • risks circularity (the virtuous person does the right thing because they are virtuous).
