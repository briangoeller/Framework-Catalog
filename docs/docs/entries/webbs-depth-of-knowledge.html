Webb's Depth of Knowledge

Also known as: DoK

framework · education · organizing-schema

Four levels of cognitive complexity: recall, skill/concept, strategic thinking, extended thinking.

Webb's Depth of Knowledge (DoK) was developed by Norman Webb at the Wisconsin Center for Education Research, articulated in his 1997 monograph Criteria for Alignment of Expectations and Assessments in Mathematics and Science Education and elaborated in subsequent work. Webb developed DoK as an alignment tool — a way to evaluate whether assessment items and curriculum standards demanded the same cognitive complexity — distinguishing four levels: DoK 1 (Recall and Reproduction — facts, definitions, simple procedures); DoK 2 (Skills and Concepts — applying procedures, summarizing, classifying with multiple steps); DoK 3 (Strategic Thinking — non-routine reasoning, justification, multiple solution paths, supporting claims with evidence); DoK 4 (Extended Thinking — investigations or research extending across multiple sessions, connecting and applying ideas across content areas). The framework gained substantial adoption in US K-12 education through state and federal alignment requirements (No Child Left Behind, subsequent ESSA), with state assessment programs and curriculum frameworks frequently citing DoK levels as required complexity ranges. DoK is often paired or compared with Bloom's Taxonomy — they classify different things (DoK measures cognitive demand of the task; Bloom classifies cognitive process type), but in practice educators often use them similarly with substantial overlap.

Core components

  • Four DoK levels: DoK 1 (Recall and Reproduction)
  • DoK 2 (Skills and Concepts)
  • DoK 3 (Strategic Thinking)
  • DoK 4 (Extended Thinking)
  • Focus on cognitive demand of task rather than student response (vs SOLO)
  • Alignment-tool origin (matching assessment to standards)
  • Distinction from Bloom's process typology
  • Adoption in US K-12 alignment frameworks
  • Often paired with Bloom in practice

Primary use case

K-12 curriculum and assessment alignment in US education contexts; state assessment design and review; teacher professional development on cognitive demand of tasks; lesson and assessment design; foundation for many state standards-alignment documents; complement to Bloom's Taxonomy in instructional design.

Common criticisms

  • Often confused with Bloom's Taxonomy despite measuring different things — DoK measures task demand while Bloom classifies cognitive process type
  • classification of items into DoK levels is subjective, with different reviewers coding the same item differently
  • DoK 3 vs DoK 4 boundary is particularly contested
  • primarily a US K-12 framework with limited international adoption
  • commercial alignment-software industry produces bureaucratic compliance with DoK distributions ('your assessment must include 20% DoK 3 items') without substantive engagement
  • like Bloom, can become checklist application rather than substantive cognitive-demand analysis
  • DoK 4 (Extended Thinking) is rarely achievable in standardized assessment contexts which constrain its practical use.
