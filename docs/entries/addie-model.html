ADDIE Model ⚑

framework · education · organizing-schema

Instructional design: Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate.

The ADDIE Model is the most widely-used instructional-design framework, structuring training and curriculum development through five sequential phases: Analyze (learner needs, audience, context, gaps, constraints); Design (learning objectives, sequencing, assessment strategy, instructional strategies); Develop (creation of materials, content, media, prototypes); Implement (delivery to learners, instructor training, infrastructure); Evaluate (formative evaluation throughout and summative evaluation after delivery, often via Kirkpatrick's four levels). The model's origins are not from a single author — it emerged from Florida State University's Center for Educational Technology in 1975 in work for the US Army's interservice training development, with no specific 'ADDIE inventor' claim. The original five-phase military model was iterative and contained substructure within each phase; the simplified linear ADDIE familiar in current corporate training was substantially developed through the 1990s. ADDIE has been the dominant framework in corporate training, military training, and higher education instructional design for decades, with Rapid Instructional Design (RID), Successive Approximation Model (SAM), and Agile Learning Design as more recent alternatives that respond to ADDIE's perceived rigidity and Waterfall-style sequencing.

Originators

Florida State University's Center for Educational Technology (1975 ISD model for US Army); broader instructional-systems-design tradition; subsequent simplification by various practitioners medium

Year / Decade

1975 (original FSU/Army ISD); 1990s (simplified linear ADDIE); ongoing use medium

Primary sources

Branson, R.K. et al. (1975). Interservice Procedures for Instructional Systems Development (FSU/Army origin), Gagne, R.M., Wager, W.W., Golas, K.C. & Keller, J.M. (2005). Principles of Instructional Design (5th ed.) medium

Core components

  • Five phases: Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate
  • Sequential default flow with iterative variants
  • Connection to Kirkpatrick's four levels of training evaluation (Reaction, Learning, Behavior, Results)
  • Subsumes specific tasks: needs analysis, learner analysis, learning objectives, instructional strategies, materials production, delivery, assessment
  • Substantial commercial training and consulting ecosystem
  • Distinction from agile-style alternatives (SAM, AGILE-LD)

Primary use case

Corporate training and L&D programs; military training development; higher-education course design; foundation for instructional-design certification (ATD, CPLP, others); reference framework in instructional-design education; basis for many learning-management-system content-development workflows.

Common criticisms

  • Sequential 'Waterfall' default produces design before implementation feedback, leading to costly rework when delivery reveals design problems
  • rigid phase structure ill-suited to rapidly-changing content (technology training, fast-evolving domains)
  • evaluation as final phase under-emphasizes formative evaluation throughout development
  • substantial documentation overhead
  • the 'ADDIE inventor' is genuinely unclear and many adoptions invoke the framework as if it were a specific designer's intentional system
  • Successive Approximation Model (Allen 2003) and Agile Learning Design respond to perceived rigidity with iterative, prototype-based alternatives
  • commercial certification industry has produced compliance-style ADDIE practice that obscures the underlying flexibility.
