Adlerian Individual Psychology

framework · psychology · organizing-schema

Adler's framework emphasizing inferiority, social interest, and lifestyle.

Alfred Adler's Individual Psychology, developed after his 1911 break from Freud's psychoanalytic movement and presented in works including Understanding Human Nature (1927) and Social Interest: A Challenge to Mankind (1933), reframed psychoanalytic theory around social embeddedness, conscious goal-directedness, and the holistic indivisibility of the person. Central concepts include: feelings of inferiority as a universal developmental experience driving compensation and growth; the inferiority complex as pathological compensation; the superiority striving as healthy compensation aimed at competence and contribution; lifestyle (Lebensstil) as the unique pattern of beliefs, goals, and behaviors that emerges in early childhood and organizes adult life; social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) as the criterion of mental health — the capacity for cooperative engagement with others and contribution to community welfare; and the encouraging vs discouraging stance in counseling and parenting. Adlerian thought has had substantial indirect influence (visible in CBT's emphasis on cognition, in humanistic psychology, in birth-order pop psychology, and recently in the resurgent self-help interest in Adler via Kishimi and Koga's The Courage to Be Disliked) though it has remained somewhat outside mainstream empirical psychology.

Primary sources

Band notes

Influential in counseling traditions; limited contemporary empirical research.

Core components

  • Feelings of inferiority and compensation
  • Inferiority complex (pathological)
  • Superiority striving (healthy)
  • Lifestyle / life style (Lebensstil) — unique organizing pattern of beliefs and goals
  • Social interest (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) as mental-health criterion
  • Birth order influences on personality
  • Family constellation
  • Encouraging counseling stance
  • Goal-directed view of behavior
  • Holism (indivisible person)

Primary use case

Counseling and psychotherapy traditions emphasizing social embeddedness and goal-directedness; school and parent education (Dreikurs' Children: The Challenge); influence on humanistic and cognitive therapies; coaching and self-development; popular self-help via Kishimi and Koga's The Courage to Be Disliked; integration with positive-psychology and meaning-centered approaches.

Common criticisms

  • Limited contemporary peer-reviewed empirical research on Adlerian therapy outcomes compared to CBT-family approaches
  • birth-order claims have weak empirical support in modern research
  • some Adlerian concepts (lifestyle, social interest) are difficult to operationalize for measurement
  • framework often subsumed into other approaches without explicit acknowledgment
  • commercial training through Adlerian institutes varies in rigor
  • can become moralistic when 'social interest' is invoked normatively without engaging the framework's full analytical content
  • works less well as a discrete intervention package than as a conceptual orientation.
