After Action Review

Also known as: AAR

practice · management · organizing-schema

Structured reflection on what was intended, what happened, why, and what to do next.

The After Action Review is a structured team-debrief practice originated by the US Army in the 1970s as part of training reform at the National Training Center, formalized in Army doctrine (TC 25-20) and subsequently adopted across military branches and adapted broadly to corporate, fire-service, healthcare, and educational contexts. The canonical structure addresses four questions: What was intended (the plan, objectives, expected outcomes)? What actually happened (observable events, in chronological order, without blame)? Why did it happen (analysis of contributing factors, decisions, environmental conditions)? What can we do better (specific lessons and recommendations for next time)? The Army emphasized AARs as ostensibly egalitarian — the lowest-ranking soldier present is encouraged to speak as freely as the senior officer — and as focused on learning rather than performance evaluation, distinctions that organizational adopters have struggled to preserve.

Originators

United States Army (1970s training reform); subsequent codification in Army doctrine high

Year / Decade

1970s development; 1981 first formal Army doctrine; 1993 TC 25-20 high

Primary sources

US Army (1993). TC 25-20: A Leader's Guide to After-Action Reviews, Garvin, D.A. (2000). Learning in Action: A Guide to Putting the Learning Organization to Work high

Core components

  • Four core questions: What was intended? What actually happened? Why did it happen? What can we do better?
  • Egalitarian voice (junior members speak freely)
  • Learning-focused (not performance evaluation)
  • Facilitator role separate from team leader
  • Specific actionable lessons
  • Distinction between formal and informal AARs

Primary use case

Team learning after operations, projects, exercises, or significant events; foundation for organizational learning practices; standard tool in military, fire service, emergency response, healthcare incident review, and increasingly corporate settings.

Common criticisms

  • Effectiveness depends heavily on psychological safety — without it, AARs become blame exercises or performative ritual
  • corporate adoption often loses the egalitarian-voice principle when senior leaders dominate
  • learnings frequently captured but rarely operationalized into practice changes
  • can become routine and lose rigor over repeated cycles
  • military origin doesn't directly translate to creative or knowledge-work contexts
  • risk of focusing on what's easy to discuss rather than the hardest issues
  • tendency to surface individual failings rather than systemic causes.
