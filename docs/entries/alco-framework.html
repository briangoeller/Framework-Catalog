ALCO Framework ⚑

Also known as: Asset-Liability Committee

framework · governance and compliance · organizing-schema

Bank governance framework for managing interest rate risk, liquidity, and balance sheet structure.

The ALCO framework refers to the governance committee and supporting analytical processes through which a bank manages the joint behavior of its assets and liabilities, focusing on interest rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB), liquidity, funding, and balance-sheet structure. ALCO emerged in US banking practice in the 1970s as rate volatility exposed the limits of separately-managed asset and funding operations, and is now standard at virtually all banks though structure varies widely by size and complexity. Typical responsibilities include setting IRR and liquidity risk appetite, reviewing economic value of equity (EVE) and net interest income (NII) sensitivity, approving funds transfer pricing (FTP) methodology, monitoring concentration and contingency funding, and reviewing investment portfolio strategy. Regulatory expectations are set by interagency IRR and liquidity guidance and the Basel Committee's IRRBB principles.

Band notes

Conceptual governance framework rather than a codified standard; specific structure varies considerably across institutions.

Core components

  • ALCO committee charter and membership
  • Risk appetite for IRR and liquidity
  • Net interest income (NII) sensitivity
  • Economic value of equity (EVE) sensitivity
  • Funds transfer pricing (FTP)
  • Liquidity coverage and contingency funding
  • Investment portfolio policy
  • Capital planning interface

Primary use case

Bank governance for joint management of interest rate risk, liquidity risk, and balance sheet structure; bridge between treasury, finance, and risk functions.

Common criticisms

  • Highly variable structure across institutions limits cross-firm benchmarking
  • can be overshadowed by treasury or risk functions, blurring ownership
  • SVB collapse in 2023 highlighted ALCO failures around duration risk and held-to-maturity portfolio classification
  • FTP design is genuinely difficult and inconsistent
  • behavioral assumptions for non-maturity deposits are often poorly validated.
