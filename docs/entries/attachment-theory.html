Attachment Theory

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Bowlby and Ainsworth's framework of early bonds shaping internal working models of relationships.

Attachment Theory was developed by British psychiatrist John Bowlby through his trilogy Attachment and Loss (1969, 1973, 1980) and substantially extended empirically by Mary Ainsworth's Strange Situation methodology, which classified infant-caregiver attachment patterns into Secure, Insecure-Avoidant, Insecure-Resistant/Ambivalent, and (later, Main and Solomon) Disorganized. Bowlby integrated psychoanalytic clinical insight with ethological observation (Lorenz's imprinting work, Harlow's primate research) and emerging cognitive psychology to argue that infants form internal working models of self and other based on early caregiver responsiveness, which shape adult relationships across the lifespan. Adult attachment research, building on Hazan and Shaver's 1987 application to romantic relationships, has produced substantial evidence linking attachment styles to relational, emotional, and health outcomes. Attachment theory is among the most empirically productive frameworks in developmental and clinical psychology, with active extensions including attachment-based therapies and attachment-informed parenting and education.

Originators

John Bowlby (foundational theory); Mary Ainsworth (Strange Situation empirical methodology); Mary Main (Disorganized classification, Adult Attachment Interview) high

Year / Decade

1969-1980 (Bowlby trilogy); 1978 (Ainsworth Patterns of Attachment); 1987 (Hazan-Shaver adult attachment) high

Primary sources

Bowlby, J. (1969, 1973, 1980). Attachment and Loss (3 vols), Ainsworth, M., Blehar, M., Waters, E. & Wall, S. (1978). Patterns of Attachment, Hazan, C. & Shaver, P. (1987). 'Romantic Love Conceptualized as an Attachment Process', JPSP high

Core components

  • Internal working models of self and other
  • Attachment behaviors organized around secure base / safe haven
  • Strange Situation paradigm
  • Four infant attachment classifications (Secure, Insecure-Avoidant, Insecure-Resistant, Disorganized)
  • Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)
  • Adult attachment dimensions (anxiety, avoidance) or styles (secure, dismissing, preoccupied, fearful)
  • Cross-generational transmission

Primary use case

Developmental psychology and early-childhood research; clinical psychology and psychotherapy (attachment-based therapies, EFT couples therapy); parenting interventions; adult relationship research and couples therapy; social work and child protection assessment; influence on educational and trauma-informed practice.

Common criticisms

  • Cross-cultural validity of Strange Situation classifications contested — distribution differs systematically across cultures (Japan, Israel kibbutz, Germany), raising questions about universal interpretation
  • deterministic readings overstate continuity from infant to adult attachment, which is moderate rather than strong
  • attachment-based parenting movements sometimes import normative claims beyond what evidence supports
  • Disorganized category is heterogeneous and difficult to assess reliably
  • risks oversimplifying complex relational dynamics into four-category typology
  • classification stability over time is moderate, not absolute.

Siblings
