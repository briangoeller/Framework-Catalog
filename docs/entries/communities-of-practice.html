Communities of Practice

Also known as: CoP

framework · knowledge management · organizing-schema

Wenger's framework: groups sharing concern and deepening expertise through interaction.

Communities of Practice (CoP) is the foundational framework articulated by Étienne Wenger (with Jean Lave's earlier collaborative work) for understanding how groups of people sharing a domain of concern learn together through sustained interaction — substantially shaping understanding of organizational learning, professional development, and informal knowledge transfer. The framework descends from Lave and Wenger's 1991 Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation (substantially analyzing how newcomers learn through participation in established practices, with the central concept of 'legitimate peripheral participation' describing how learners gradually move from peripheral to central engagement) and was substantially codified through Wenger's foundational 1998 Communities of Practice: Learning, Meaning, and Identity. Wenger identified three substantial defining characteristics: (1) Domain — shared area of interest distinguishing the community from other groupings; (2) Community — sustained interaction, mutual engagement, and relationships among members; (3) Practice — shared repertoire of resources (experiences, stories, tools, ways of addressing recurring problems). CoPs differ substantially from teams (organized around tasks with formal accountability), networks (broader connections without practice focus), and communities of interest (shared topic without practice). Communities of practice exist in organizations (engineering practices, professional communities within firms, cross-departmental practitioner communities), professions (academic disciplines, medical specialties, legal practice areas), and civil society (online forums, advocacy groups, religious practices). Wenger's substantial subsequent work (Communities of Practice and Social Learning Systems 2000, Cultivating Communities of Practice with McDermott and Snyder 2002, Digital Habitats with White and Smith 2009) substantially extended the framework into organizational practice and digital contexts. CoPs have been substantially adopted in knowledge-management practice through corporate community programs (Shell, World Bank, NASA, US Army Center for Lessons Learned), with substantial commercial knowledge-management infrastructure built around community concepts. Critics including Stephen Roberts and others note that commercial CoP adoption varies substantially in fidelity to Wenger's substantive framework — many corporate 'communities of practice' are essentially scheduled meetings without substantive sustained practice or organic community formation.

Core components

  • Three defining characteristics: Domain, Community, Practice
  • Distinction from teams, networks, communities of interest
  • Legitimate peripheral participation (Lave-Wenger 1991)
  • Sustained interaction and mutual engagement
  • Shared repertoire of resources
  • Application across organizations, professions, civil society
  • Substantial subsequent extension to digital habitats and online communities
  • Substantial corporate knowledge-management adoption (Shell, World Bank, NASA, US Army)
  • Connection to broader situated-learning tradition
  • Distinction from formal organizational structures

Primary use case

Foundational framework in organizational learning and knowledge management; basis for substantial corporate community programs across multinational organizations; reference framework in knowledge-management education globally; foundation for substantial knowledge-sharing and professional-development programs; integration with broader organizational-learning frameworks; pedagogical foundation in organizational behavior and management education; influence on online community design, professional association programming, education research; foundation for substantial commercial knowledge-management consulting practice; substantial application in international development (World Bank), military (US Army Center for Lessons Learned), and humanitarian sectors.

Common criticisms

  • Commercial CoP adoption varies substantially in fidelity to Wenger's substantive framework — many corporate 'communities of practice' are essentially scheduled meetings or email distribution lists without substantive sustained practice or organic community formation
  • the framework's substantial commercial popularization has produced 'community of practice' as buzzword applied to almost any group with shared interest
  • integration with formal organizational structures is genuinely tense — communities of practice often work best when relatively informal, but corporate adoption typically formalizes them in ways that may compromise their organic character
  • cross-cultural variation in community formation is substantial — Wenger's framework reflects particular Western organizational culture that doesn't always travel cleanly
  • commercial knowledge-management software has produced compliance-style 'community' tools (corporate social networks, expertise-location systems) with varying analytical fidelity to the substantive framework
  • tendency for CoP rhetoric to coexist with substantively top-down knowledge-management practices that contradict the framework's organic-community commitments
  • the original Lave-Wenger framework was substantially descriptive of how learning happens in apprenticeship and naturally-occurring practice
  • subsequent Wenger work substantially shifted toward prescriptive guidance for cultivating communities, raising questions about whether deliberately-cultivated communities have the same dynamics as naturally-occurring ones
  • integration with virtual and distributed work (which has substantially grown since 2020) requires substantial adaptation
  • community-of-practice programs face substantial measurement challenges — substantial knowledge-management research has documented difficulty demonstrating ROI of CoP investments.
