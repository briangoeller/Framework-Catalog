Connectivism

framework · education · organizing-schema

Siemens' learning theory for the digital age emphasizing networks of nodes.

Connectivism was articulated by George Siemens in his 2004 essay 'Connectivism: A Learning Theory for the Digital Age' (and elaborated by Stephen Downes in subsequent work) as a learning theory positioned for digital, networked learning environments. The framework's central claim is that learning is the process of forming and traversing networks of connections — among neurons, among ideas, and among people, technologies, and information sources — and that knowledge resides in the networks rather than in individual minds alone. Siemens articulates principles including: learning may reside in non-human appliances; nurturing and maintaining connections is needed to facilitate continual learning; ability to see connections between fields, ideas, and concepts is a core skill; currency (accurate up-to-date knowledge) is the intent of all connectivist learning activities. Connectivism emerged contemporaneously with the early MOOCs (Siemens and Downes designed the first connectivist MOOC, CCK08, in 2008), and the framework substantially shaped early thinking about online networked learning. Whether connectivism is a genuinely new learning theory or a reinterpretation of social and distributed-cognition frameworks within a digital context remains contested — critics including Verhagen (2006) have argued that connectivism does not meet the criteria for a learning theory but is rather an instructional or curriculum approach.

Originators

George Siemens; Stephen Downes (substantial collaborator) high

Year / Decade

2004 (Siemens essay); 2005-2008 (substantial development including first cMOOC) high

Primary sources

Siemens, G. (2004). 'Connectivism: A Learning Theory for the Digital Age' (elearnspace.org), Downes, S. (2005). 'An Introduction to Connective Knowledge', Verhagen, P. (2006). 'Connectivism: A New Learning Theory?' (critique) high

Core components

  • Learning as network-formation and traversal
  • Knowledge in networks (not just minds)
  • Eight principles of connectivism (Siemens 2004)
  • Distinction from constructivism (knowledge construction in mind)
  • Connection to digital and networked learning
  • Foundation for connectivist MOOCs (cMOOCs)
  • Currency (up-to-date knowledge) as goal
  • Decision-making as itself a learning process

Primary use case

Theoretical framework for online and networked learning; foundation for connectivist MOOC design; influence on Personal Learning Networks (PLN) and Personal Learning Environments (PLE); reference framework in digital education research; basis for some emerging online and lifelong-learning practices.

Common criticisms

  • Verhagen (2006) 'Connectivism: A New Learning Theory?' argued connectivism does not meet criteria for a learning theory (no account of learning mechanism at the individual level) and is better understood as an instructional approach
  • critics including Bell (2009) argue connectivism is reinterpretation of distributed cognition, social constructivism, and network theory rather than novel
  • the framework's emphasis on currency over depth has been argued to underweight the value of canonical knowledge that doesn't change rapidly
  • commercial MOOC platforms (Coursera, edX) have largely been xMOOCs (broadcast model) rather than cMOOCs, suggesting connectivism's pedagogical implications have not driven mainstream adoption
  • integration with cognitive science is limited
  • substantial overlap with social constructivism in practice
  • works principally as digital-age framing rather than as substantively new theory.
