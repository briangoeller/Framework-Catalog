Cornell Note-Taking

tool · education · organizing-schema

Note format dividing the page into cues, notes, and summary sections.

The Cornell Note-Taking System was developed by Walter Pauk at Cornell University in the 1950s, presented in his 1962 How to Study in College (multiple subsequent editions). The format divides each notebook page into three sections: a wide right-hand column (about 6 inches) for taking notes during class or reading; a narrow left-hand column (about 2.5 inches) used afterward for cue-words, questions, and key terms drawn from the notes; and a bottom section (about 2 inches) for a brief summary of the page's content written within 24 hours after the original notes. The system's pedagogical structure encourages active engagement with material at three points: during the lecture or reading (notes column); shortly after, when reviewing notes to identify cues (cue column); and within 24 hours, when summarizing the page (summary section). Subsequent review involves covering the notes column and using the cue column to test recall — operationalizing active recall and spaced review on the same physical page. The system has been substantially adopted in college study-skills programs and in K-12 secondary education, and remains the most-recognized named note-taking format. Empirical research on Cornell-specific outcomes is limited but the underlying practices (active engagement, cue-based review, summarization) are well-supported.

Primary sources

Core components

  • Three-section page layout: notes column (right, ~6 inches), cue column (left, ~2.5 inches), summary section (bottom, ~2 inches)
  • Notes during class/reading
  • Cues drawn from notes after class
  • Summary written within 24 hours
  • Review by covering notes and using cues for active recall
  • Connection to active recall and spaced review
  • Application across disciplines
  • Adapts to digital implementations (Notion, OneNote templates)

Primary use case

College and high-school study skills; self-directed learning and lecture note-taking; basis for many university study-skills curricula; foundation for many digital note-taking templates; integration with active-recall and spaced-review study workflows.

Common criticisms

  • Empirical research on Cornell-specific outcomes is limited — the underlying practices (active engagement, summarization, cue-based review) have substantial evidence, but the specific three-section format isn't necessarily superior to other formats that incorporate these practices
  • the format works best for content that fits linear note-taking, less well for highly-visual or spatial content (math proofs, diagrams, system architecture)
  • the cue-and-summary discipline is often skipped in practice, reducing the format to ordinary linear notes
  • rigid format can constrain note-taking flexibility for content that doesn't fit the structure
  • digital implementations vary in fidelity to the original system's discipline
  • commercial study-skills industry has produced compliance-style adoption
  • works better with structured lectures than with discussion-based or laboratory contexts.
