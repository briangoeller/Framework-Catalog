Crossing the Chasm

Also known as: Technology Adoption Lifecycle

framework · marketing · organizing-schema

Moore's adaptation showing the gap between early adopters and early majority.

Crossing the Chasm was articulated by Geoffrey A. Moore in his 1991 book of the same name (revised editions through 2014), substantially extending Everett Rogers's Diffusion of Innovations adopter categories to high-tech product marketing. Moore's central insight was that Rogers's smooth S-curve obscures a critical discontinuity for technology products: a chasm exists between the Early Adopters (visionaries who buy on the basis of strategic potential, willing to tolerate immature products) and the Early Majority (pragmatists who buy on the basis of practical proven value, demanding mature products with established references). Many high-tech products gain initial traction with early adopters but fail to cross into mainstream market acceptance because the marketing approaches that work for early adopters (vision-selling, strategic partnership, technology-first) don't work for the early majority (who want references, complete products, and risk reduction). Moore's prescription: focus on a specific niche segment within the early majority where you can become the dominant solution (the 'beachhead' or 'D-Day strategy'), build whole-product completeness for that segment, accumulate references, and use those references to attack adjacent segments (the 'bowling alley'). The framework substantially shaped Silicon Valley product-marketing thinking from the 1990s onward and remains a foundational reference in B2B technology marketing, despite criticism that the chasm metaphor doesn't apply equally to all product categories and that successful technology companies often don't follow the bowling-alley prescription as Moore described.

Year / Decade

Primary sources

Moore, G.A. (1991, 1999, 3rd ed. 2014). Crossing the Chasm: Marketing and Selling Disruptive Products to Mainstream Customers, Moore, G.A. (1995). Inside the Tornado high

Core components

  • The chasm: discontinuity between Early Adopters (visionaries) and Early Majority (pragmatists)
  • Different buying psychology: visionaries vs pragmatists
  • Beachhead strategy: dominate one specific niche segment first
  • Whole-product completeness for the niche
  • Reference-driven adjacent-segment expansion (bowling alley, then tornado in subsequent Moore books)
  • Connection to Rogers's Diffusion of Innovations
  • High-tech product context
  • Inside the Tornado (1995) and Living on the Fault Line (2000) as Moore's subsequent extensions

Primary use case

B2B and high-tech product marketing; foundation for substantial Silicon Valley marketing thinking; basis for go-to-market strategy in technology startups and growth-stage companies; reference framework in technology product management; influence on venture-capital evaluation of startup go-to-market plans; integration with broader product-strategy frameworks; pedagogical reference in technology marketing education.

Common criticisms

  • Chasm metaphor doesn't apply equally to all product categories — consumer products often don't show the discontinuity Moore identified
  • successful technology companies often don't follow the bowling-alley prescription as Moore described, raising questions about whether the framework is descriptive or normative
  • Ehrenberg-Bass empirical research challenges the underlying targeting-based growth model that Crossing the Chasm shares with broader STP
  • specific historical examples Moore used have been argued to be retrofit rather than predictive
  • commercial application sometimes confuses 'crossing the chasm' with simply 'going from startup to growth stage' without engaging the specific buying-psychology distinction
  • the framework's age (1991) means it predates SaaS, freemium, product-led growth, and other contemporary go-to-market models that don't fit the original framing
  • Moore's subsequent books (Tornado, Fault Line) have varied reception suggesting the original Chasm framework was unusually robust
  • many B2B technology companies fail to cross the chasm despite explicit Chasm-based strategy, suggesting the framework identifies a real challenge but doesn't fully solve it.
