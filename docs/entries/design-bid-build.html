Design-Bid-Build

framework · engineering · doctrinal-institutional

Traditional construction project delivery method with sequential design, competitive bidding, and construction phases under separate contracts.

Design-Bid-Build (DBB) is the traditional construction-delivery method in which the project owner sequentially (1) contracts with an architect-engineer to produce complete design documents, then (2) competitively bids construction work to general contractors based on those completed documents, then (3) contracts with the lowest responsible bidder for construction. The method's three discrete phases (design, bid, build) and separation of design and construction responsibilities give the method its name. DBB has substantial historical foundation in US public-sector procurement, where competitive low-bid principles became codified through the late-19th and 20th centuries to constrain corruption, ensure fair competition, and provide cost-reasonableness assurance. It remains the dominant delivery method for US public-sector construction in many jurisdictions, though alternative-delivery methods (CMAR, Design-Build, IPD) have substantially eroded DBB market share since the 1990s. AIA Document A101 (Owner-Contractor Agreement) and A201 (General Conditions) provide standard DBB contract forms.

Originators

Diffuse historical development through US public-sector procurement evolution (late-19th through 20th century); Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) framework, state and local procurement statutes (with substantial variation across jurisdictions); American Institute of Architects (AIA) contract-document tradition, particularly A101 Owner-Contractor Agreement (originally 1888 'Standard Form of Agreement Between Owner and Contractor'); intellectual antecedents in 19th-century anti-corruption procurement reform (US federal procurement evolution post-Civil War), Progressive-Era public-administration reform, broader public-procurement-as-public-trust tradition high

Core components

  • Three sequential phases: design (architect-engineer produces complete documents), bid (competitive bidding of construction), build (construction execution by lowest responsible bidder)
  • Sequential procurement structure: design contract first, construction contract after design completion
  • Architect-engineer role: responsible for design completeness, accuracy, and constructability
  • typically provides construction-administration services during construction phase but does not bear construction execution risk
  • General contractor role: bears full construction-execution risk based on completed design documents
  • subcontracts trades through competitive subcontract bidding
  • Owner role: holds two separate contracts (designer and contractor)
  • manages design-construction interface
  • absorbs design-error risk that produces construction change orders
  • Competitive low-bid selection: typically lowest bid from responsible bidders meeting bid-package requirements (public-sector)
  • private-sector permits more selection discretion
  • Bid-package documents: drawings, specifications, general conditions, supplementary conditions, bid form, instruction to bidders
  • Change-order mechanism: contractual process for addressing design errors, owner-directed scope changes, and unforeseen conditions during construction
  • Disputes resolution: AIA dispute-resolution provisions, construction-industry dispute review boards, mediation, arbitration, litigation
  • AIA contract documents: A101 Owner-Contractor Agreement, A201 General Conditions of the Contract for Construction

Primary use case

Dominant historical delivery method for US public-sector construction across federal, state, and local jurisdictions; default delivery method for public-procurement statutes in many US states absent specific authorization for alternative-delivery methods; applied principally in: highway and transportation infrastructure (state DOTs, federal-aid highways), K-12 school districts, water and wastewater infrastructure, general-purpose government buildings; private-sector use for projects where design completeness before bidding is feasible and price-competitive selection is desired; academic and professional reference in construction management, public-procurement, and project-management literature; comparative reference framework in alternative-delivery methodology research (CMAR, DB, IPD performance frequently benchmarked against DBB).

Common criticisms

  • DBB's adversarial dynamics have been substantially documented in construction-industry research — the structural separation of design and construction responsibilities, combined with low-bid contractor selection, produces incentives for change-order exploitation by contractors, design-error blame-shifting between contractors and designers, and adversarial owner-contractor relationships that drive cost and schedule overruns
  • the lack of constructability input from contractors during design produces design-stage decisions that prove costly or impractical during construction, requiring expensive change orders that low-bid contractors are economically motivated to exploit
  • sequential phase structure produces longer total project duration than overlapping-phase methods (CMAR, DB, IPD) — the design must be complete before bidding can proceed
  • substantial empirical evidence (Konchar-Sanvido 1998 foundational comparison, El Asmar et al. 2013, multiple subsequent studies) documents DBB performance disadvantages compared to alternative-delivery methods on cost growth, schedule growth, and quality metrics — though DBB advocates argue selection bias toward complex projects in alternative-delivery samples confounds the comparison
  • low-bid selection produces 'race-to-the-bottom' dynamics where qualified contractors lose to underqualified or aggressive-bidding competitors, with subsequent project-execution failures
  • the public-procurement justification (corruption prevention through competitive low-bid) has been argued to be insufficient justification given documented poor outcomes and the existence of alternative procurement frameworks (best-value selection, qualifications-based selection) that retain corruption-prevention while improving project outcomes
  • integration with modern construction tools (BIM, lean construction, prefabrication) is awkward — DBB's sequential design-completion-then-construction structure does not accommodate iterative design-construction integration that BIM and lean construction enable
  • ongoing erosion of DBB market share to alternative methods reflects industry consensus about DBB's limitations even where public-procurement statutes preserve DBB as default.
