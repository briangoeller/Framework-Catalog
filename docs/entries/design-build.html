Design-Build

framework · engineering · doctrinal-institutional

Construction project delivery method consolidating design and construction responsibilities under a single contract.

Design-Build (DB) is the construction-delivery method in which a single entity (the design-builder, typically a contractor with in-house design capability or a contractor-designer joint venture) provides both design and construction services under a single contract with the owner. Compared to traditional Design-Bid-Build, DB consolidates design and construction responsibility, permits design-construction overlap, and shifts design-error risk from owner to design-builder. The method has ancient antecedents (master builders combined design and construction roles before professional separation in the 19th-20th centuries) but modern DB is institutionally associated with the Design-Build Institute of America (DBIA, founded 1993) which has substantially developed standard contracts, training, and certification infrastructure. DB has grown substantially since the 1990s — DBIA's Design-Build Done Right research documents DB market share growth in non-residential construction. Public-sector adoption was accelerated by 1996 federal Clinger-Cohen Act and state-level enabling legislation through the 2000s-2010s. Variants include Progressive Design-Build (with collaborative price development) and fixed-price Design-Build.

Originators

Core components

  • Single-entity responsibility: design-builder provides both design and construction under single contract
  • Design-builder structures: contractor with in-house design capability (most common)
  • contractor-designer joint venture
  • designer-led design-build (less common)
  • Owner relationships: owner has single contract with design-builder
  • owner's typical roles include programming, design review, construction monitoring (often through owner's representative or program manager)
  • Project delivery variants: Progressive Design-Build (collaborative design and price development through milestones), fixed-price Design-Build (lump sum after RFP/proposal phase), Bridging (owner develops preliminary design then competes DB), Engineering-Procurement-Construction (EPC) for industrial projects
  • Selection methods: best-value selection (qualifications and price), low-bid selection (less common in DB), qualifications-based selection (some progressive-DB and professional-services-style)
  • Risk allocation: design-error risk shifted to design-builder (substantial contrast with DBB)
  • construction-execution risk on design-builder
  • site-conditions and differing-conditions risk varies by contract
  • Performance specifications versus prescriptive specifications: DB facilitates performance-based specifications enabling design-builder innovation
  • AIA contract documents: A141 Owner-Design-Builder, A142 Design-Builder-Subcontractor
  • DBIA contract documents and supplemental forms
  • Common application contexts: transportation infrastructure (state DOTs), federal facilities, healthcare, industrial EPC projects, prefabricated and modular construction

Primary use case

Construction-delivery method with substantial growth in US construction since 1990s, applied principally in: transportation infrastructure (highway, bridge, transit projects under state DOT and FHWA frameworks); federal government construction (post-Clinger-Cohen Act facilitation); healthcare facility construction; industrial and energy projects (typically through EPC variant); performance-driven projects where owner specifies outcomes rather than design specifics; schedule-critical projects benefiting from design-construction overlap; private-sector commercial construction; academic and professional reference in construction management, project management, and public-procurement literature; DBIA certification, training, and professional infrastructure; international diffusion through global EPC contracting tradition and local design-build adaptations.

Common criticisms

  • DB's consolidation of design and construction responsibility under a single entity has been substantially debated — supporters argue this aligns incentives and reduces design-construction interface conflicts, while critics argue it eliminates the independent-designer check-and-balance that protects owner interests against contractor cost-cutting decisions
  • the design-builder's economic incentive to produce designs that minimize construction cost may produce designs less optimized for owner's long-term operational and life-cycle interests, with documented concerns about value engineering decisions favoring contractor margin over owner interests
  • performance specifications enable design-builder innovation but also enable design-builder shortcuts that performance criteria do not adequately constrain
  • quality-control infrastructure differs from DBB — DBB's independent designer provides ongoing construction-administration review, while DB's same-entity design-construction structure produces less independent oversight, which owners typically address through owner's representative or independent peer review at substantial additional cost
  • selection methodology is contested — best-value selection (qualifications plus price) is broadly preferred but its specific scoring criteria can be manipulated, while low-bid selection produces 'race-to-the-bottom' similar to DBB
  • substantial empirical evidence (Konchar-Sanvido 1998, El Asmar et al. 2013, FHWA studies) documents DB performance advantages over DBB on cost growth, schedule growth, and unit cost — though selection bias and project-complexity confounds limit confident generalization
  • public-procurement enabling legislation varies substantially across US states, producing fragmented DB landscape
  • small-business and local-business participation can be reduced under DB structure since only large integrated firms can typically respond
  • owner-program-manager and owner-representative roles have grown substantially as compensation for the reduced independent-design oversight DB inherently provides, producing additional cost layer
  • integration with sustainability requirements (LEED, energy codes) and accessibility codes places substantial design-builder responsibility for compliance that may produce documentation gaps
  • ongoing growth of Progressive Design-Build addresses some traditional DB concerns while producing its own contractual complexity.
