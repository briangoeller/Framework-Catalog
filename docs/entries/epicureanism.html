Epicureanism

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Hellenistic philosophy seeking tranquility through prudent pleasure and absence of pain.

Epicureanism was founded by Epicurus (341-270 BCE) in his Athenian school, the Garden, and substantially preserved through the Roman poet Lucretius's De Rerum Natura (On the Nature of Things, 1st century BCE) and the surviving fragments of Epicurus's writings. The system integrates atomistic physics (drawn from Democritus, modified by Epicurus's swerve to allow for free will), an empiricist epistemology (sensations and prolepses as criteria of truth), and a hedonist ethics with substantial nuance: pleasure is indeed the goal of life, but the highest pleasure is ataraxia (tranquility, freedom from anxiety) and aponia (absence of bodily pain), achieved not through maximal sensory indulgence but through prudent moderation, friendship, philosophical understanding, and the elimination of unfounded fears (especially fear of death and fear of the gods). Epicurus's tetrapharmakos (fourfold remedy) summarizes: God is not to be feared; death is nothing to us (we are not present to experience our nonexistence); good is easy to attain; evil is easy to endure. Epicureanism declined after the rise of Christianity (its materialism and rejection of providential gods made it unpalatable) and was rediscovered through Lucretius in the Renaissance, with substantial influence on early modern atomism, materialism, and Enlightenment thought.

Originators

Epicurus (founder); Metrodorus, Hermarchus (early followers); Lucretius (Roman expositor and preserver) high

Year / Decade

~307 BCE (Epicurus founds the Garden); 1st century BCE (Lucretius); Renaissance rediscovery via Lucretius high

Primary sources

Epicurus. Letters to Herodotus, Pythocles, and Menoeceus, Principal Doctrines, Vatican Sayings (surviving fragments), Lucretius. De Rerum Natura (On the Nature of Things), Greenblatt, S. (2011). The Swerve: How the World Became Modern (cultural history) high

Core components

  • Atomism (atoms and void as fundamental)
  • The swerve (clinamen, allowing free will)
  • Empiricist epistemology (sensations and prolepses)
  • Pleasure as ethical goal, but ataraxia (tranquility) as highest pleasure
  • Aponia (absence of pain)
  • Tetrapharmakos (fourfold remedy)
  • Distinction between necessary, natural-but-not-necessary, and vain desires
  • Death is nothing to us (no experiencer)
  • Friendship as central good
  • Withdrawal from political life ('live unnoticed')

Primary use case

Foundational Hellenistic philosophical school; major historical influence on materialist and naturalist traditions; influence on Lucretian atomism, early modern philosophy (Hobbes, Gassendi), and Enlightenment thought (Diderot, Jefferson); contemporary practical-philosophy interest alongside Stoicism revival; foundation for some contemporary discussions of well-being and minimalism.

Common criticisms

  • Caricatured throughout history as crude hedonism (the modern colloquial 'epicurean' inverts the original meaning) — Epicurus actually advocated simple pleasures and warned against indulgence
  • popular reception has consistently failed to engage the framework's atomistic-epistemological substructure
  • argument that 'death is nothing to us' (since we're not present to experience our death) is contested by responses including the deprivation account (death deprives us of future goods)
  • withdrawal from political life ('live unnoticed') sits awkwardly with civic obligations
  • tetrapharmakos's optimistic 'evil is easy to endure' is hard to maintain in cases of severe suffering
  • Christian opposition produced enduring negative reception in much of Western tradition
  • less prominent contemporary revival than Stoicism despite arguably similar therapeutic potential.
