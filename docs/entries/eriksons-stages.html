Erikson's Stages

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Eight psychosocial stages from trust vs mistrust through integrity vs despair.

Erik Erikson's psychosocial development theory, presented in Childhood and Society (1950) and further elaborated in subsequent works including Identity: Youth and Crisis (1968), reframed Freud's psychosexual stages as a lifespan sequence of psychosocial crises, each pairing a developmental challenge with its potentially adverse outcome: Trust vs Mistrust (infancy), Autonomy vs Shame and Doubt (early childhood), Initiative vs Guilt (preschool), Industry vs Inferiority (school age), Identity vs Role Confusion (adolescence), Intimacy vs Isolation (young adulthood), Generativity vs Stagnation (middle adulthood), and Integrity vs Despair (late adulthood). Successful resolution of each stage produces a corresponding 'virtue' (hope, will, purpose, competence, fidelity, love, care, wisdom) and lays foundation for subsequent stages, while unresolved crises constrain later development. Erikson's contribution of identity development as a central adolescent task influenced developmental psychology substantially, and his lifespan framing — extending development past childhood — was distinctive at the time. Modern developmental research has supported broad outlines while questioning the strict stage progression and universal applicability.

Year / Decade

1950 (Childhood and Society); 1968 (Identity: Youth and Crisis); 1982 (lifespan extension with Joan Erikson) high

Primary sources

Erikson, E.H. (1950). Childhood and Society, Erikson, E.H. (1968). Identity: Youth and Crisis, Erikson, E.H. (1982). The Life Cycle Completed high

Core components

  • Eight psychosocial stages: Trust/Mistrust, Autonomy/Shame, Initiative/Guilt, Industry/Inferiority, Identity/Role Confusion, Intimacy/Isolation, Generativity/Stagnation, Integrity/Despair
  • Each stage with associated virtue (hope, will, purpose, competence, fidelity, love, care, wisdom)
  • Crisis resolution as developmental currency
  • Lifespan rather than child-only frame
  • Identity status extension by James Marcia (achievement, foreclosure, moratorium, diffusion)

Primary use case

Foundation of lifespan developmental psychology; teaching framework in developmental and counseling psychology; clinical orientation for assessing developmental tasks across the lifespan; integration with psychotherapy formulation; influence on identity development research particularly in adolescence.

Common criticisms

  • Strict age-stage progression not consistently observed empirically — many people work on multiple psychosocial tasks simultaneously and revisit earlier stages
  • cross-cultural validity contested, particularly the identity stage which assumes Western individualistic self-construction
  • gender critiques (Carol Gilligan and others) of the male-default development picture in early articulation
  • clinical case-study evidence dominated initial formulation
  • identity-status extension (Marcia) is more empirically rigorous than original Erikson formulation
  • stage virtues are normative claims dressed in developmental language
  • doesn't well integrate with cognitive and neurobiological development frameworks.
