Experiential Learning

Also known as: Kolb's Cycle

framework · education · organizing-schema

Learning cycle: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, active experimentation.

David Kolb's Experiential Learning Theory was articulated in Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development (1984), drawing on the work of Kurt Lewin, John Dewey, and Jean Piaget to characterize learning as the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience. The framework presents a four-stage cycle: Concrete Experience (CE — engaging in a specific activity or experience); Reflective Observation (RO — reviewing and reflecting on the experience); Abstract Conceptualization (AC — drawing conclusions, forming concepts, integrating with prior understanding); Active Experimentation (AE — applying the new understanding in subsequent action). The cycle is iterative — each new experience initiates another round through the four stages. Kolb additionally articulated four learning styles based on individual preferences for combining the dimensions: Diverging (CE+RO), Assimilating (AC+RO), Converging (AC+AE), and Accommodating (CE+AE), assessed through the Learning Style Inventory (LSI). The experiential learning cycle has substantial support and is widely used in adult education, corporate training, and experiential-education programs. The associated learning-styles taxonomy, however, has been largely disconfirmed by mainstream cognitive-psychology research — Pashler et al.'s 2009 'Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence' (Psychological Science in the Public Interest) reviewed the evidence and concluded that matching instruction to claimed learning styles does not improve outcomes. The cycle and the styles must be evaluated separately.

Band notes

Kolb's experiential cycle has more support than the associated learning-styles taxonomy, which has been largely disconfirmed.

Core components

  • Four-stage cycle: Concrete Experience (CE), Reflective Observation (RO), Abstract Conceptualization (AC), Active Experimentation (AE)
  • Iterative cycle through experiences
  • Two dimensions: grasping experience (CE-AC) and transforming experience (RO-AE)
  • Four learning styles (Diverging, Assimilating, Converging, Accommodating) — empirically contested
  • Learning Style Inventory (LSI)
  • Connection to Lewin's action research
  • Distinction between experiential cycle (more supported) and learning styles (largely disconfirmed)

Primary use case

Adult education and corporate training; experiential-education programs (outdoor education, internships, service learning); foundation for reflective-practice frameworks in professional education (medicine, nursing, education, social work); leadership-development programs; basis for many design-thinking and learning-by-doing methodologies; component of David Boud's reflection-on-experience work in higher education.

Common criticisms

  • Kolb's learning-styles taxonomy has been largely disconfirmed empirically — Pashler et al. 2009 and subsequent work have shown that matching instruction to claimed learning styles does not improve learning outcomes, despite continuing popularity
  • the experiential cycle's four-stage sequence is empirically less rigid than presented (learners often skip stages or revisit non-sequentially)
  • the LSI's psychometric properties have been challenged
  • commercial certification and assessment industry around Kolb's framework has produced widespread use of the learning-styles claims despite the disconfirming research
  • the cycle is genuinely useful as a framework for thinking about reflective practice, but the popular conflation of cycle-and-styles damages its credibility
  • cultural variation in experience-reflection norms underweighted in original framing.
