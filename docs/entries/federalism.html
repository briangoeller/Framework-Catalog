Federalism

framework · political science · organizing-schema

Sovereignty divided constitutionally between national and subnational governments.

Federalism is the constitutional system dividing sovereignty between a central (national, federal) government and constituent (state, provincial, regional) governments, with both levels having direct authority over citizens within their respective spheres of competence rather than the central government acting only through subnational governments (confederalism) or the subnational governments existing only at the central government's pleasure (unitary states with administrative devolution). The framework descends from the practical political problem of combining multiple polities into larger unions — the Articles of Confederation of the United States (1781-1789) demonstrated the weaknesses of confederalism, prompting Madison, Hamilton, Jay, and others to design the federal system embedded in the US Constitution and defended in the Federalist Papers (1787-1788). Subsequent federal systems include Canada (1867), Australia (1901), Germany (1949), India (1950), Brazil, Switzerland, and many others, with substantial variation in: the allocation of competences between levels (enumerated powers vs residual powers, exclusive vs concurrent competences); fiscal arrangements (revenue assignments, equalization, transfers); judicial enforcement (supreme court jurisdiction over federal-subnational conflicts); and political integration (party systems aligned vs distinct across levels). Theoretical work distinguishes 'dual federalism' (separate spheres, less cooperation), 'cooperative federalism' (substantial inter-level coordination), 'fiscal federalism' (Wallace Oates's economic theory of optimal allocation of public functions across levels), and 'asymmetric federalism' (different subnational units with different competences, e.g., Quebec in Canada, Catalonia in Spain, Hong Kong in China). Federalism faces substantial contemporary stress from globalization (some functions move up to international bodies), urbanization (cities acquiring importance the framework didn't anticipate), and identity-based pressures (regional independence movements).

Core components

  • Constitutional division of sovereignty between national and subnational governments
  • Both levels with direct authority over citizens
  • Distinction from confederalism (subnational primacy) and unitary systems (central primacy with administrative devolution)
  • Allocation of competences (enumerated, residual, exclusive, concurrent)
  • Fiscal federalism (revenue assignment, equalization, transfers)
  • Judicial enforcement of inter-level boundaries
  • Variants: dual, cooperative, fiscal, asymmetric federalism
  • Connection to checks and balances
  • Substantial cross-national variation

Primary use case

Constitutional design for diverse polities; foundation for substantial work in comparative politics and federalism studies; reference framework in EU integration debates (sui generis but with federal characteristics); basis for analyses of subnational variation in policy outcomes; foundation for fiscal federalism in public economics; integration with broader constitutional theory; pedagogical reference in political science, law, and public administration; influence on devolution debates in unitary states.

Common criticisms

  • Federal systems can produce inefficient duplication, race-to-the-bottom dynamics in regulation, and coordination failures across levels
  • substantive critique that federalism in some contexts (US South historically) has protected subnational illiberal practices against national rights protections — the federalism-vs-civil-rights tension
  • cooperative federalism has eroded the distinct-spheres model in many federal systems, producing 'marble cake' rather than 'layer cake' federalism (Morton Grodzins) that complicates accountability
  • fiscal federalism in practice often produces unfunded mandates and revenue centralization despite spending decentralization
  • asymmetric federalism creates equity questions about why some subnational units have different competences than others
  • supranational integration (EU) and globalization pressure traditional federal arrangements
  • cities have grown more important than the framework anticipated
  • commercial federalism literature varies substantially in analytical fidelity
  • framework can be invoked rhetorically to oppose national policies regardless of their merits
  • specific allocation of competences involves substantial path-dependence and political-coalition negotiation that the framework doesn't fully theorize.
