Fishbone Diagram

Also known as: Ishikawa Diagram, Cause-and-Effect Diagram

tool · management · organizing-schema

Visual root-cause analysis grouping causes under categories like people, process, materials.

The Fishbone Diagram, developed by Kaoru Ishikawa at Kawasaki Shipyards in the 1960s and named for its visual resemblance to a fish skeleton, is a structured cause-and-effect analysis tool that organizes potential causes of a defined effect (the fish's head) into categorical 'bones' branching off the spine. The classic 6M categories for manufacturing are Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, and Mother Nature (environment), with service-industry adaptations like 4P (Policies, Procedures, People, Plant) and 8P variants. The diagram supports systematic brainstorming by ensuring causes from multiple categories are considered, and is one of the seven basic tools of quality (alongside Pareto charts, control charts, histograms, scatter diagrams, check sheets, and stratification or flow charts). It is typically used in conjunction with Five Whys to drill down from category-level causes to root causes.

Core components

  • Effect (the problem) at the head
  • Major cause categories as primary bones
  • Sub-causes as secondary bones
  • 6M categories (Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Mother Nature) for manufacturing
  • 4P/8P variants for services
  • Pairs with Five Whys for drill-down
  • One of seven basic tools of quality

Primary use case

Root-cause analysis in quality improvement, manufacturing, healthcare incident review, and service operations; structured brainstorming for incident postmortems; teaching tool for systematic causal reasoning; component of A3 problem-solving and DMAIC analyze phase.

Common criticisms

  • Generates lists of potential causes without prioritizing or testing which are actually contributing
  • categorical structure can force-fit causes that don't naturally belong
  • tendency to populate all categories even when some are not relevant
  • doesn't address interactions between causes
  • static snapshot rather than dynamic system view
  • can substitute for empirical investigation
  • outcome quality depends heavily on team breadth and brainstorming rigor
  • works better for well-bounded operational problems than for systemic or organizational issues.
