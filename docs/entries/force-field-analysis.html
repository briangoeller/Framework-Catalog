Force Field Analysis

tool · management · organizing-schema

Lewin's mapping of driving and restraining forces around a proposed change.

Force Field Analysis, developed by Kurt Lewin as part of his broader field-theory and change-model work in the 1940s, maps the forces favoring (driving) and opposing (restraining) a proposed change around a current equilibrium state. The analytical premise is that change can be achieved either by strengthening driving forces, weakening restraining forces, or both — and that Lewin generally argued weakening restraining forces is preferable because strengthening driving forces tends to provoke increased opposition (the system pushes back against perceived pressure). The graphical convention places driving forces as arrows pushing against restraining-force arrows, with arrow length or weight representing relative strength. The tool pairs naturally with Lewin's three-stage Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze model: force field analysis identifies what must shift in the unfreezing stage. It remains a staple of organizational development and change-management practice.

Primary sources

Lewin, K. (1947). 'Frontiers in Group Dynamics', Human Relations, Lewin, K. (1951). Field Theory in Social Science high

Core components

  • Current state at center
  • Driving forces (arrows pushing toward desired change)
  • Restraining forces (arrows pushing back)
  • Force strength shown by arrow length or weight
  • Lewin's preference for weakening restraining over strengthening driving forces
  • Equilibrium framing
  • Pairs with Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze

Primary use case

Change management diagnostic and planning; structured analysis of organizational change readiness; strategic planning for change initiatives; teaching tool in organizational development; complement to other change-management frameworks.

Common criticisms

  • Static snapshot of a dynamic system — forces shift over time as the change unfolds
  • strength estimates are subjective and politically loaded
  • treats forces as discrete when they often interact
  • can become a brainstorming exercise without driving real action
  • like Lewin's broader change model, the equilibrium framing assumes a stable starting point that may not exist in continuous-change environments
  • doesn't address how to actually weaken restraining forces (the key prescriptive move) beyond identification.
