Great Man Theory

framework · history · philosophical-tradition

Carlyle's view of history as fundamentally the biography of exceptional individuals; widely critiqued and largely abandoned in academic history.

Great Man Theory is the historiographical and broader social-theoretical view that history is fundamentally driven by exceptional individuals — great political leaders, generals, prophets, and intellectual figures — whose decisions and personal qualities shape the course of events. The view was articulated most influentially by Thomas Carlyle in his 1840 London lectures published as On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History (1841), with Carlyle famously asserting that 'the history of the world is but the biography of great men.' The theory has been substantially abandoned in academic historiography since the late 19th century, displaced first by structural and economic explanations (including but not limited to Marxist historiography), then by social-history and history-from-below traditions. Herbert Spencer's The Study of Sociology (1873) provided the foundational structural critique. However, biographical and political-leadership-centered history has persisted (notably in popular historical writing) and recent scholarship in fields including military history and intellectual history has rehabilitated more nuanced individual-agency analysis.

Originators

Thomas Carlyle (foundational systematic articulation, On Heroes 1841); antecedents in classical biographical historiography (Plutarch, Suetonius), Renaissance humanism, and 18th-century 'great-figures' tradition; Herbert Spencer (1873 foundational structural-critique); 20th-century rehabilitation in nuanced forms by Sidney Hook (The Hero in History 1943) and others high

Year / Decade

1840-1841 (Carlyle's lectures and publication); 1873 (Spencer's critique); ongoing presence in popular history high

Primary sources

Carlyle, T. (1841). On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History, Spencer, H. (1873). The Study of Sociology, Hook, S. (1943). The Hero in History: A Study in Limitation and Possibility, Tolstoy, L. (1869). War and Peace (second epilogue, philosophical critique) high

Core components

  • Foregrounding of exceptional individuals as principal historical agents
  • Biographical narrative structure focused on leaders, prophets, generals, innovators
  • Implicit or explicit hero-worship framework in Carlyle's original formulation
  • Rejection of structural, economic, and impersonal-force explanations
  • Carlyle's six categories: Hero as Divinity, Prophet, Poet, Priest, Man of Letters, King
  • Sidney Hook's distinction between 'eventful' and 'event-making' figures (more nuanced 20th-century reformulation)
  • Continuation in popular biographical history and political-leadership studies

Primary use case

Largely abandoned in academic historiography but operates as a discipline-internal warning category against biographical-reductionist history; Continuing presence in popular historical writing, biographical history, and political-leadership studies; Reference framework in historiography curricula illustrating 19th-century historiographical positions and the structural-history displacement that followed; Subject of ongoing debate about whether structural-history overcorrected by underweighting individual agency.

Common criticisms

  • The empirical adequacy of Great Man Theory was substantially challenged by Spencer's 1873 critique arguing that 'great men' arise out of and are shaped by their society's prior development, with Tolstoy's lengthy polemic against great-man-historical interpretation in War and Peace (1869, particularly the second epilogue) providing influential literary expression of the same argument
  • Marxist and structural-history traditions made comprehensive structural critiques across the 19th and 20th centuries, with E.H. Carr's What Is History? (1961) providing a widely-cited summary
  • counterfactual analysis (Geoffrey Hawthorn, Niall Ferguson) has been used both to support and to challenge great-man claims about specific historical events
  • the tradition's gender bias (the persistent focus on 'great men' to the exclusion of women) is now substantially documented in feminist historiography (Joan Kelly, Joan Scott, Bonnie Smith)
  • the tradition's Eurocentrism produced systematic neglect of non-Western political and intellectual leadership
  • recent work in intellectual history (Quentin Skinner's Cambridge School), military history (Geoffrey Parker), and political-leadership studies has rehabilitated more nuanced individual-agency analysis without returning to Carlyle's hero-worship framing
  • popular and political rhetoric continues to deploy great-man framings (the 'Founding Fathers' in US political culture, charismatic-leadership claims in authoritarian regimes) that academic historiography continues to critique.
