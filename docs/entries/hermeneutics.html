Hermeneutics

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Theory and practice of interpretation, especially of texts and historical understanding.

Hermeneutics is the theory and practice of interpretation, with roots in biblical and legal exegesis traditions extending back to antiquity. Modern philosophical hermeneutics emerged through Friedrich Schleiermacher's general hermeneutics in the early 19th century (extending interpretive methods beyond sacred texts to all language), Wilhelm Dilthey's distinction between explanation (in natural sciences) and understanding (Verstehen, in human sciences), Martin Heidegger's ontological turn that made interpretation a fundamental mode of being-in-the-world (Being and Time, 1927), and most influentially Hans-Georg Gadamer's Truth and Method (1960), which articulated hermeneutic experience as fundamental to human understanding generally. Key concepts include the hermeneutic circle (understanding part requires understanding whole and vice versa); fusion of horizons (interpretation as encounter between interpreter's horizon and text's horizon); effective historical consciousness (recognition that interpretation occurs within tradition); and the productive role of prejudice (Vorurteil, prior understanding) in interpretation. Paul Ricoeur's hermeneutics of suspicion vs hermeneutics of trust (engaging Marx, Nietzsche, Freud) and Jurgen Habermas's critique of Gadamer (insisting on critical reflection on tradition) extend the tradition. Hermeneutics is foundational to continental philosophy and has substantial influence in literary theory, theology, legal interpretation, and qualitative research methodology.

Schleiermacher, F. (1819 lectures, posthumous). Hermeneutics, Dilthey, W. (1900). 'The Rise of Hermeneutics', Gadamer, H.-G. (1960). Truth and Method, Ricoeur, P. (1981). Hermeneutics and the Human Sciences high

Core components

  • Hermeneutic circle (whole and parts)
  • Fusion of horizons (Gadamer)
  • Effective historical consciousness
  • Productive role of prejudice/prior understanding
  • Distinction between explanation (natural science) and understanding (human science)
  • Hermeneutic of suspicion vs hermeneutic of trust (Ricoeur)
  • Phronesis as hermeneutic virtue
  • Habermas's emphasis on critical reflection on tradition
  • Application to texts, history, art, social practices, and self-understanding

Primary use case

Continental philosophy and philosophy of human sciences; literary theory and criticism; theology and biblical interpretation; legal interpretation theory; qualitative research methodology in social sciences; foundation for some approaches to historical understanding; influence on philosophical anthropology and cultural theory.

Common criticisms

  • Habermas-Gadamer debate raised substantive concerns about hermeneutics' conservatism — Gadamer's emphasis on tradition can underweight the role of critical reflection in escaping ideological distortion
  • relativism concerns: if all interpretation is horizon-bound, can we adjudicate between competing interpretations?
  • hermeneutic-circle structure has been argued to risk circular reasoning when applied without methodological discipline
  • integration with empirical methods is genuinely difficult
  • tradition's denseness limits accessibility
  • competing schools (Schleiermacher's romantic hermeneutics, Dilthey's psychological, Gadamer's ontological, Ricoeur's, Betti's objectivist) create which-hermeneutics-when ambiguity
  • Anglo-American analytic philosophy has largely been skeptical of the tradition.
