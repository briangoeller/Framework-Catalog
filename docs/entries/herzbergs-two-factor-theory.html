Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Also known as: Motivation-Hygiene Theory

framework · management · organizing-schema

Distinguishes hygiene factors that prevent dissatisfaction from motivators that drive satisfaction.

Frederick Herzberg's two-factor theory, presented in The Motivation to Work (1959, with Bernard Mausner and Barbara Snyderman) and elaborated in his 1968 HBR article 'One More Time: How Do You Motivate Employees?', argues that workplace satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of a single continuum but separate dimensions driven by different factors. Hygiene factors (company policy, supervision, salary, working conditions, interpersonal relationships) prevent dissatisfaction when adequate but do not produce satisfaction when improved beyond adequate; motivator factors (achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth) produce satisfaction when present but their absence does not produce dissatisfaction. The framework's prescriptive consequence is that improving hygiene factors does not motivate — only enriching the work itself can. Herzberg's KITA framework (Kick In The Ass) and job enrichment recommendations followed directly. Subsequent empirical research has consistently failed to replicate the two-factor structure when alternative methodologies are used, raising questions about whether the structure was an artifact of Herzberg's specific 'critical incident' interview method.

Band notes

Influential but the two-factor structure has not been replicated in subsequent research.

Core components

  • Hygiene factors (extrinsic): company policy, supervision, salary, working conditions, interpersonal relations
  • Motivator factors (intrinsic): achievement, recognition, work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth
  • Asymmetric effects (hygiene prevents dissatisfaction, motivators produce satisfaction)
  • KITA critique of incentive approaches
  • Job enrichment as prescription

Primary use case

Job design and enrichment programs; foundational reference in compensation strategy debates; teaching tool for distinguishing extrinsic from intrinsic motivation.

Common criticisms

  • Methodological critique: the two-factor structure may be an artifact of the critical-incident interview method (people attribute good experiences to themselves and bad ones to environment)
  • subsequent studies using different methods consistently fail to replicate the two-factor finding
  • salary classification as 'hygiene' contradicts substantial behavioral evidence on compensation effects
  • cultural and individual variation in what counts as motivator vs hygiene
  • treats motivation as universal when expectancy and self-determination theories provide better-validated alternatives
  • binary categorization oversimplifies factor effects.
