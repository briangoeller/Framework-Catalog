Heuristics and Biases Program

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Kahneman and Tversky's research program cataloguing systematic deviations from rational judgment.

The Heuristics and Biases Program was launched by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's collaboration beginning in 1969, with foundational papers including 'Belief in the Law of Small Numbers' (1971), 'Subjective Probability: A Judgment of Representativeness' (1972), and 'Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases' (1974). The program cataloged systematic deviations of human judgment from the predictions of normative probability theory and decision theory, identifying mental shortcuts (heuristics) that often produce useful judgments but that systematically fail in identifiable ways (biases). Three foundational heuristics were articulated: Availability (judging probability by ease of recall), Representativeness (judging category membership by similarity to category prototype), and Anchoring and Adjustment (estimates pulled toward initial reference points). Subsequent decades expanded the catalog substantially — confirmation bias, hindsight bias, framing effects, base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy, and dozens more. The program produced Prospect Theory (1979), influenced behavioral economics decisively, and earned Kahneman the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economics (Tversky having died in 1996).

Core components

  • Availability heuristic (probability judgment by ease of recall)
  • Representativeness heuristic (similarity to category prototype)
  • Anchoring and adjustment
  • Many subsequent biases including confirmation, hindsight, framing, base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, planning fallacy, optimism bias
  • Methodology: comparison of human judgment to normative standards
  • Connection to Prospect Theory and Dual-Process Theory
  • Distinction between System 1 (heuristic) and System 2 (analytic) processing

Primary use case

Foundation of behavioral economics; basis for judgment-and-decision-making research; debiasing interventions in clinical, financial, medical, and intelligence-analysis contexts; foundation for nudge and choice-architecture work; influence on product and policy design; basis for critical-thinking education.

Common criticisms

  • Replication crisis in social psychology has affected several specific findings cited prominently in Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow (notably some priming effects)
  • Gigerenzer and the 'fast and frugal heuristics' school (ABC research group) argue that heuristics are often ecologically rational rather than systematically biased, and that comparing human judgment to normative probability theory is the wrong yardstick
  • debiasing interventions show modest and inconsistent effects
  • bias catalog has grown to a point that almost any judgment can be labeled some bias post-hoc
  • limited integration with neurobiological accounts of decision-making
  • cross-cultural variation in some biases is substantial.
