History from Below

Also known as: People's History

framework · history · philosophical-tradition

Historical approach reconstructing the experiences and agency of ordinary people, workers, and marginalized groups rather than elites.

History from Below is the historiographical approach reconstructing the experiences, agency, and consciousness of ordinary people, workers, and marginalized groups rather than political and social elites. The approach was pioneered in the British Marxist Historians tradition of the mid-20th century, with E.P. Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class (1963) as the canonical exemplar — Thompson explicitly framed his project as rescuing the 'poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the obsolete hand-loom weaver' from 'the enormous condescension of posterity.' Earlier French antecedents include Georges Lefebvre's work on the French Revolutionary peasantry and the histoire des sans-culottes tradition; subsequent international development includes Howard Zinn's A People's History of the United States (1980), the broader people's-history tradition, and interconnections with Subaltern Studies, social history, women's history, and the history of slavery and resistance. The approach transformed labor history, peasant history, and the social history of early-modern and modern Europe, North America, and beyond.

Core components

  • Reorientation of historical attention from elites to working classes, peasants, slaves, women, and marginalized groups
  • Source-base expansion: pamphlets, ballads, court records, oral history, material culture, anonymous-author records
  • Recovery of agency: subjects as active historical participants rather than passive objects of elite action
  • Class-formation analysis: Thompson's working-class as 'made' through experience and consciousness rather than economic position alone
  • Methodological combination of social, cultural, economic, and political history
  • Engagement with contemporary politics (Thompson's New Left commitments, Zinn's anti-war politics)
  • Connections to subaltern studies, women's history, history of slavery and resistance

Primary use case

Foundational approach for labor history, working-class history, peasant history, and history of slavery and resistance; research framework for social history of early-modern and modern Europe, North America, and beyond; intellectual foundation for women's history, history of sexuality, and subaltern history; pedagogical canon in social history and historiography curricula; popular history audience through Howard Zinn-style accessible synthesis; engagement with contemporary social movements and political education.

Common criticisms

  • History from Below has been substantially debated within its own tradition and from outside — the linguistic-and-cultural turn in history (Patrick Joyce Visions of the People 1991, Class 1995
  • Geoff Eley and Keith Nield 'Why Does Social History Ignore Politics?' 1980, subsequent work) challenged Thompson's framework for naturalizing class identity rather than recognizing it as discursively constructed
  • the tradition's gender blindness in foundational work was substantially documented by feminist historians (Joan Scott 'Women in The Making of the English Working Class' 1983, Anna Clark The Struggle for the Breeches 1995), prompting both substantial critique and subsequent incorporation
  • the working-class focus produced inadequate attention to other axes of marginalization (race, sexuality, disability) addressed by subsequent generations
  • the empirical scope of Thompson's and successors' work has been challenged on selected-evidence grounds
  • Zinn-style popular extensions have been argued by some historians to trade analytical depth for accessibility, with Wilfred McClay and others questioning the historical-pedagogical adequacy of A People's History
  • the approach's political commitments (Thompson's New Left, Zinn's activism) produce ongoing debate about whether engaged history compromises historical objectivity, with substantial pushback from engaged historians arguing that supposedly-neutral elite history has its own equally-political commitments
  • relationship to contemporary neoliberal economic and political conditions has prompted new debates about working-class formation in 21st-century contexts.
