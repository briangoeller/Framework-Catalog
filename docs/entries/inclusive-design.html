Inclusive Design

framework · design · organizing-schema

Designing for the full range of human diversity rather than the average user.

Inclusive Design is the design philosophy and methodology that explicitly designs for the full range of human diversity — including but not limited to ability, age, language, culture, gender, and other dimensions of difference — rather than for an idealized 'average' or 'normal' user. The framework was substantially developed through the British Standards Institution's BS 7000-6 (2005, Design management systems — Managing inclusive design), Microsoft's Inclusive Design Toolkit (2015, with substantial subsequent development), and academic-practice work at the Helen Hamlyn Centre for Design at the Royal College of Art (founded 1999). The framework's central commitments include: design that works well for one user often works less well for others; designing from the margins (with users typically excluded by mainstream design) often produces better solutions for everyone (the 'curb cut effect' — sidewalk ramps designed for wheelchair users benefit cyclists, parents with strollers, delivery workers); exclusion is the result of mismatched design rather than user limitation (Microsoft's 'Persona Spectrum' framework distinguishes permanent, temporary, and situational disability — someone with one arm permanently, with an arm injury temporarily, or carrying a baby situationally all face similar interface challenges). Inclusive Design overlaps substantially with Universal Design (separately enriched) but with substantively different framings: Universal Design aims to design single solutions usable by all; Inclusive Design accommodates diversity through multiple paths or adaptations. Both descend from disability-rights and accessibility traditions and connect to broader work on human-centered design, equity in design, and design justice (Sasha Costanza-Chock 2020). Inclusive Design has substantial commercial adoption particularly in technology (Microsoft, Apple, Google have substantial inclusive-design programs) and in public services.

Core components

  • Design for full range of human diversity
  • Multiple paths or adaptations rather than single universal solution
  • Persona Spectrum (permanent, temporary, situational disability — Microsoft framework)
  • Curb cut effect (designs from margins benefit everyone)
  • Distinction from but overlap with Universal Design
  • Connection to disability rights and accessibility traditions
  • Foundation in broader Human-Centered Design
  • Application to technology, public services, physical environments
  • Connection to design justice (Costanza-Chock 2020) and equity-in-design movements

Primary use case

Foundation for accessible and inclusive design across technology, public services, physical environments; basis for substantial work at Microsoft, Apple, Google, and other technology companies; reference framework for accessibility regulation and standards (Americans with Disabilities Act compliance, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines); integration with broader human-centered design; pedagogical foundation in design education; influence on product strategy and inclusive market expansion; basis for substantial consulting practice.

Common criticisms

  • Implementation varies substantially across organizations — substantial gap between inclusive-design rhetoric and actual product accessibility for many users with disabilities
  • commercial 'inclusive design' marketing sometimes overstates what products actually accomplish
  • tension between Inclusive Design's accommodation framing (multiple paths) and Universal Design's universal-solution framing (single solution for all) creates which-when ambiguity in practice
  • tendency to focus on disability dimensions at expense of other diversity dimensions (cultural, linguistic, socioeconomic) that the broader framework includes
  • design-justice critique (Costanza-Chock and others) that inclusive design has been substantially co-opted by corporate interests in ways that obscure ongoing exclusion of disabled users from design decision-making
  • integration with quantitative accessibility testing is often weak
  • cross-cultural variation in disability experience and accessibility norms is substantial
  • some applications use 'inclusive' as language without substantively different practice
  • tension between inclusive-design ideals and substantial commercial pressure for fast cheap design
  • works better when championed throughout development than as late-stage compliance check.
