Job Characteristics Model

Also known as: Hackman-Oldham Model

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Hackman and Oldham's framework relating five job characteristics (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, feedback) to motivation and performance through critical psychological states.

The Job Characteristics Model (JCM, also Hackman-Oldham Model) is the framework relating five core job characteristics to motivation, satisfaction, and performance through three intermediate critical psychological states, articulated by J. Richard Hackman and Greg Oldham in their 1975 Organizational Behavior and Human Performance article and 1976 Job Diagnostic Survey, and consolidated in their 1980 book Work Redesign. The five job characteristics are skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback (from the work itself). These produce three critical psychological states: experienced meaningfulness of the work (from the first three characteristics), experienced responsibility for outcomes (from autonomy), and knowledge of actual results (from feedback). These states then produce outcomes including high internal work motivation, high-quality performance, high job satisfaction, and low absenteeism and turnover. The Motivating Potential Score (MPS) formula combines the five characteristics multiplicatively. JCM extends Frederick Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory by specifying which job-design features produce intrinsic motivation.

Core components

  • Five core job characteristics: skill variety (range of different activities and skills required), task identity (degree to which the job involves completing a whole identifiable piece of work), task significance (impact on lives of others), autonomy (freedom and discretion in scheduling and methods), feedback (direct information about performance from the work itself)
  • Three critical psychological states: experienced meaningfulness of work, experienced responsibility for work outcomes, knowledge of actual results
  • Motivating Potential Score (MPS) = ((Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance) / 3) × Autonomy × Feedback (multiplicative formula)
  • Outcome variables: internal work motivation, growth satisfaction, general job satisfaction, work effectiveness
  • Moderator variables: growth-need strength (individual preference for challenge and growth), knowledge and skill (ability to perform the job), context satisfaction (satisfaction with pay, supervision, co-workers)
  • Job Diagnostic Survey: 21-item self-report measure of the five characteristics, three states, and outcomes
  • Job-redesign implications: enriching jobs by increasing the five characteristics through combining tasks, forming natural work units, establishing client relationships, vertical loading, opening feedback channels

Primary use case

Foundational framework in organizational behavior, industrial-organizational psychology, and human-resource management; applied framework for job design, job enrichment, and work-redesign interventions; academic reference and pedagogical canon in OB and IO psychology curricula; research instrument (Job Diagnostic Survey) for empirical studies of work characteristics and outcomes; intellectual foundation for subsequent work-design research including Job Demands-Resources Model, Self-Determination Theory applications to work, and Adam Grant's relational job-design extensions.

Common criticisms

  • JCM has substantial empirical support (Fried and Ferris's 1987 meta-analysis of approximately 200 studies
  • subsequent Humphrey, Nahrgang, and Morgeson 2007 meta-analysis extending and refining), but the multiplicative MPS formula has weak empirical justification — additive combinations of the five characteristics typically perform as well or better in predicting outcomes
  • the three critical psychological states have been argued to be substantially redundant with the job characteristics themselves, with some research suggesting the states do not provide independent explanatory value beyond the characteristics
  • growth-need strength as moderator has produced inconsistent empirical results across studies
  • the Job Diagnostic Survey's psychometric properties have been refined through subsequent instruments (Idaszak and Drasgow's revised JDS, the Work Design Questionnaire by Morgeson and Humphrey 2006) addressing factor-structure concerns
  • cross-cultural applicability has produced mixed results, with the framework performing differently in collectivist versus individualist cultures and in different regulatory and labor-market contexts
  • the framework's individual-job-level focus underweights team-level and organizational-level work-design considerations that subsequent sociotechnical and team-design research has emphasized
  • emergent workforce features (gig work, remote work, AI-augmented work) raise questions about the model's continued applicability that recent research is beginning to address
  • the model's normative implications (jobs should be enriched) face implementation difficulties in actual organizational contexts where economic, technological, and labor-relations constraints limit job-redesign options
  • Adam Grant's relational job-design research has substantially extended the task-significance dimension by emphasizing beneficiary contact and pro-social motivation
  • integration with broader Self-Determination Theory frameworks has been uneven.
