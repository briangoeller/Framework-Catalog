Job Demands-Resources Model

Also known as: JD-R

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Demerouti and Bakker's framework explaining burnout and engagement at work.

The Job Demands-Resources Model was introduced by Evangelia Demerouti, Arnold Bakker, Friedhelm Nachreiner, and Wilmar Schaufeli in 2001 ('The Job Demands-Resources Model of Burnout', Journal of Applied Psychology) as a parsimonious framework integrating prior work-stress models. It holds that all working conditions can be classified into two categories: Job Demands (physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects requiring sustained effort and associated with physiological or psychological costs — e.g., workload, time pressure, emotional demands, role conflict) and Job Resources (aspects functional in achieving work goals, reducing demand cost, or stimulating growth — e.g., autonomy, feedback, social support, learning opportunities). The model's two parallel processes are an Energetic process (high demands → exhaustion → ill-health) and a Motivational process (high resources → engagement → positive outcomes), with resources buffering the impact of demands. Personal resources (self-efficacy, optimism, resilience) and job crafting were added in subsequent extensions. JD-R has accumulated substantial empirical support and is widely used in occupational health psychology and organizational practice.

Core components

  • Job Demands (physical, psychological, social, organizational)
  • Job Resources (autonomy, feedback, social support, learning opportunities)
  • Two parallel processes: Energetic (demands → exhaustion → ill-health) and Motivational (resources → engagement → positive outcomes)
  • Resources buffer demand impact
  • Personal Resources (self-efficacy, optimism, resilience)
  • Job Crafting
  • Burnout (UWES) and Work Engagement (Utrecht Work Engagement Scale) measurement

Primary use case

Occupational health psychology research; workplace burnout and engagement assessment; organizational interventions to reduce burnout and increase engagement; HR analytics and employee experience programs; foundation for many corporate wellbeing initiatives; integration with leadership development and job design programs.

Common criticisms

  • Demands-resources distinction can be context-dependent — same factor can function as either depending on perception (e.g., challenge demands vs hindrance demands)
  • reliance on self-report measures vulnerable to common-method bias
  • interactional effects (resources moderating demand impact) inconsistently observed in meta-analyses
  • cross-cultural variation in what counts as resource or demand
  • the model has expanded over time to incorporate many constructs, raising parsimony concerns
  • lacks integration with structural and political dimensions of work (power, precarity)
  • intervention research thinner than measurement research.
