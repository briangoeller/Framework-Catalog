Karma Framework

framework · theology · contemplative-tradition

Action and consequence across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions.

Karma (Sanskrit: 'action,' 'work,' 'deed') is the foundational Indian metaphysical and ethical framework that intentional actions produce consequences (phala, 'fruit') that bind their author through the cycle of rebirth (samsara), with the broad principle that wholesome actions produce favorable conditions and unwholesome actions produce unfavorable conditions, in this life or future lives. The framework is shared across (with substantive variation between) Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism — all four traditions affirm karma as central, but their philosophical accounts differ substantially. Hindu accounts (in Vedanta, Yoga, Samkhya, and other schools) typically frame karma as a metaphysical mechanism within a cosmos governed by ishvara (divine governance) or by the impersonal law of dharma, with multiple types of karma distinguished (sanchita — accumulated; prarabdha — fructifying in current life; agami/kriyamana — generated by current actions). Buddhist accounts reject the substantive self that Hindu karma seems to presuppose (anatta), reframing karma as the operation of intentional action shaping subsequent moments and lives without an enduring self that bears it; Buddhist ethics emphasizes intention (cetana) as what makes an action karmically significant. Jain accounts treat karma as a subtle physical substance that accumulates on the soul and must be purged through ascetic practice. The doctrine has substantial implications for Indian ethics, soteriology, and social thought, and has been variously received and misunderstood in Western reception (often reduced to 'what goes around comes around' without engagement with the rebirth context, and sometimes weaponized to blame victims for their circumstances).

Originators

Foundational Indian religious tradition (Vedic and pre-Vedic origins); substantial development across Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and Sikh traditions high

Year / Decade

Vedic and Upanishadic foundations (~2nd millennium-1st millennium BCE); systematic philosophical development across Indian schools; ongoing tradition medium

Primary sources

Bhagavad Gita (especially chapters 2-4 on karma yoga), Upanishads (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya), Buddhist Pali Canon (multiple suttas on kamma), Jain Tattvartha Sutra, substantial contemporary engagement across traditions high

Core components

  • Intentional action produces consequence (phala)
  • Connection to rebirth (samsara)
  • Wholesome vs unwholesome actions
  • Multiple karma types (sanchita, prarabdha, agami) in Hindu accounts
  • Buddhist emphasis on intention (cetana)
  • Jain physicalist account of karma as subtle substance
  • Distinction across Hindu schools (with ishvara-grounded vs impersonal accounts)
  • Connection to liberation (moksha/nirvana) as escape from karmic bondage
  • Distinction from determinist or fatalist readings
  • Substantial Western reception variation

Primary use case

Foundational framework in Indian philosophical and religious traditions across Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism; reference framework in Indian ethical thought; basis for substantial soteriological and ethical literature; integration with contemporary engagement with Indian thought in interfaith and comparative-religion contexts; foundation for some contemporary discussions of moral responsibility and ethics; influence on global cultural understanding of action and consequence.

Common criticisms

  • Substantive variation across traditions creates which-karma-when ambiguity in cross-tradition discussion
  • popular Western reception often reduces karma to 'what goes around comes around' without engagement with the rebirth context that gives the doctrine its specific shape
  • risk of karmic-blame readings that hold victims responsible for their suffering as 'karmic consequences' — substantial intra-tradition critique of this misuse, but it persists in popular and political reception
  • tension between karma as ethical-metaphysical framework and contemporary materialist or naturalist worldviews
  • Buddhist non-self doctrine creates philosophical challenges for the framework that Buddhist philosophers have addressed substantially
  • cross-cultural application of Indian framings is uneven
  • commercial New Age and self-help adaptations have produced varying fidelity to traditional teachings
  • tension between karma as long-term cosmic mechanism and immediate ethical guidance
  • debates within Hindu tradition about how karma operates within a theistic providential framework.
