Karpman Drama Triangle

framework · psychology · organizing-schema

Three relational roles in conflict: Persecutor, Victim, Rescuer.

The Karpman Drama Triangle was introduced by Stephen Karpman in his 1968 paper 'Fairy Tales and Script Drama Analysis' (Transactional Analysis Bulletin) within the broader Transactional Analysis tradition. The framework identifies three interlocking psychological roles people unconsciously adopt in dysfunctional interpersonal dynamics: Persecutor (the blamer or aggressor — 'It's all your fault'); Victim (the helpless one — 'Poor me'); and Rescuer (the helper — 'Let me help you,' typically without solicitation and often perpetuating dependence). All three roles avoid genuine responsibility — Persecutor blames, Victim avoids agency, Rescuer's help maintains the Victim's victim position — and individuals frequently rotate among the roles within a single interaction. Acey Choy's 1990 'Winner's Triangle' and David Emerald's 2005 'TED Empowerment Dynamic' offer healthier role alternatives (Challenger, Vulnerable, Coach in Choy; Creator, Challenger, Coach in Emerald) for clinical and coaching application. The framework is widely used clinically and in coaching, particularly because it offers a memorable diagnostic structure for relational patterns.

Band notes

Clinically useful schema; not empirically tested as a falsifiable theory.

Core components

  • Three roles: Persecutor (blamer), Victim (helpless), Rescuer (unsolicited helper)
  • Role rotation within interactions
  • All roles avoid genuine responsibility
  • Connection to Transactional Analysis games
  • Healthier alternatives: Choy's Winner's Triangle (Challenger, Vulnerable, Coach)
  • Emerald's TED Empowerment Dynamic (Creator, Challenger, Coach)
  • Diagnostic for codependent and conflict patterns

Primary use case

Couples and family therapy; coaching for managing interpersonal patterns; workplace conflict-resolution training; clinical work with codependency, addiction-related dynamics, and abusive relationships; teaching tool in TA and broader counseling training; common framework in popular self-help and relationship literature.

Common criticisms

  • Not empirically tested as a falsifiable theory — primarily a clinical schema rather than a research-grounded model
  • categorization of any conflict can be made to fit the three roles, raising falsifiability concerns
  • oversimplifies complex dynamics that involve more than three roles or different configurations
  • commercial coaching adoption sometimes uses the triangle to label normal disagreement as 'drama'
  • can foster self-righteous spotting of others' roles rather than self-reflection
  • risks pathologizing legitimate distress as 'victim role'
  • doesn't address structural and contextual contributors to apparent role behavior.
