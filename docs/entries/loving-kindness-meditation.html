Loving-Kindness Meditation

Also known as: Metta

practice · theology · contemplative-tradition

Buddhist practice cultivating benevolence toward self, loved ones, neutral parties, and difficult people.

Loving-Kindness Meditation (Pali: metta bhavana; Sanskrit: maitri bhavana) is a foundational Buddhist contemplative practice for cultivating unconditional friendliness and benevolence toward all beings. The canonical source is the Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8), in which the Buddha presents loving-kindness as a path to liberation, with the iconic image of a mother protecting her only child as the model for the boundless quality of well-wishing to be cultivated. Metta is the first of the four brahma-viharas or 'divine abodes' alongside compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita), and equanimity (upekkha). The traditional practice progresses through stages of recipient: oneself, a benefactor, a neutral person, a difficult person, and ultimately all beings without distinction, typically using phrases like 'May you be happy, may you be free from suffering, may you be at peace.' The practice is preserved across Theravada, Mahayana, and Vajrayana traditions, with substantial variation in technique. Loving-kindness practice has entered Western psychology through the broader mindfulness movement, with research by Barbara Fredrickson, Sharon Salzberg's secular teachings, and clinical adaptations including Compassion-Focused Therapy (Paul Gilbert). The Buddhist contemplative depth and the secular research-grounded adaptations represent substantively different practices despite shared vocabulary.

Originators

Year / Decade

Primary sources

Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8), Visuddhimagga (Buddhaghosa, 5th century CE) chapter on the brahma-viharas, Salzberg, S. (1995). Lovingkindness: The Revolutionary Art of Happiness, Gilbert, P. (2009). The Compassionate Mind high

Core components

  • Metta as one of four brahma-viharas (with karuna, mudita, upekkha)
  • Karaniya Metta Sutta as canonical source
  • Progressive recipients: self, benefactor, neutral person, difficult person, all beings
  • Traditional phrases of well-wishing
  • Connection to liberation in Buddhist soteriology
  • Contemporary secular adaptations through mindfulness movement
  • Compassion-Focused Therapy (Gilbert)
  • Distinction from generic positive-thinking practices

Primary use case

Buddhist contemplative practice across Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana traditions; foundation for the four brahma-viharas; preparatory and standalone practice in Buddhist monastic and lay practice; contemporary secular adaptation in mindfulness-based interventions; clinical applications including Compassion-Focused Therapy; growing presence in trauma-informed and end-of-life care contexts.

Common criticisms

  • Internal Buddhist debates about the relationship between metta as worldly state and metta as path-to-liberation
  • whether secular adaptations preserve the practice's transformative depth or reduce it to feel-good positive psychology
  • concerns within Buddhist communities about decontextualization when the practice is separated from the broader Eightfold Path and Three Trainings (sila, samadhi, panna)
  • the Western tendency to treat metta as primarily about self-compassion can elide the traditional emphasis on extending benevolence to difficult and unknown others
  • cross-tradition variation in technique creates which-version-when ambiguity
  • some teachers note that mechanical repetition of phrases without genuine intention produces limited fruit
  • contemporary popularization through commercial apps and books varies substantially in fidelity to traditional teaching.
