Marxist Historiography

Also known as: Historical Materialism (history)

framework · history · philosophical-tradition

Historical analysis treating class struggle, modes of production, and material conditions as the primary engines of historical change.

Marxist Historiography is the historical tradition treating class struggle, modes of production, and material conditions as the principal engines of historical change, deriving from Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels' own historical writings (notably Marx's The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte 1852 and the historical chapters of Capital 1867) and developed through subsequent generations of Marxist historians. The British Marxist Historians group of the mid-20th century (E.P. Thompson, Eric Hobsbawm, Christopher Hill, Rodney Hilton, George Rudé) produced particularly influential work on English working-class formation, the transition from feudalism to capitalism, and revolutionary movements. French Marxist historians Georges Lefebvre and Albert Soboul transformed French Revolution historiography. Subaltern Studies (founded 1982 by Ranajit Guha) and the Latin American Marxist historiography of dependency theorists extended the tradition to colonial and post-colonial contexts. After 1989 the tradition has substantially shifted but remains influential in labor history, world-systems analysis, and global economic history.

Originators

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels (foundational, mid-19th century); British Marxist Historians group: E.P. Thompson, Eric Hobsbawm, Christopher Hill, Rodney Hilton, George Rudé, Maurice Dobb (mid-20th century); French Marxist tradition: Georges Lefebvre, Albert Soboul; Antonio Gramsci (intellectual antecedent for Subaltern Studies); Eric Williams (Capitalism and Slavery 1944, foundational for postcolonial Marxist historiography) high

Year / Decade

1845-1867 (Marx-Engels foundational); 1940s-1960s (British Marxist Historians peak); ongoing development high

Primary sources

Marx, K. (1852). The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, Thompson, E.P. (1963). The Making of the English Working Class, Hobsbawm, E.J. (1962, 1975, 1987, 1994). The Age of Revolution / Capital / Empire / Extremes (four-volume series), Anderson, P. (1974). Lineages of the Absolutist State high

Core components

  • Modes of production and transitions between them (slave, feudal, capitalist) as principal historical periodization
  • Class struggle as engine of historical change
  • Base-superstructure relationship: economic production conditions political and ideological forms
  • Historical materialism as methodological commitment
  • Long-run analysis of capitalist development, primitive accumulation, imperialism
  • Working-class formation and revolutionary movement analysis
  • Critical analysis of bourgeois historiography
  • Engagement with structure-agency dialectic (E.P. Thompson's substantial contribution)
  • World-systems and dependency-theory extensions (Wallerstein, Frank, dos Santos)

Primary use case

Foundational framework for labor history, working-class history, history of revolutionary movements, and economic history; intellectual foundation for Subaltern Studies, History from Below, Postcolonial History; research framework for transition-from-feudalism debates, French and Russian Revolution historiography, slavery and capitalism scholarship; academic reference in global economic history, world-systems analysis, and decolonization studies; pedagogical canon in graduate-level historiography curricula.

Common criticisms

  • Marxist historiography has been substantially challenged across multiple fronts — the post-1989 period produced a substantial reassessment of Marxist historical work in light of the failure of state-socialist regimes, with Hobsbawm's continued defense of the tradition (The Age of Extremes 1994) generating substantial criticism
  • the cultural turn in history (Lynn Hunt, Joan Scott) challenged Marxist materialism's explanatory primacy, arguing class identity is itself culturally constructed rather than economically given
  • E.P. Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class (1963) was substantially critiqued by Patrick Joyce (Visions of the People 1991, Class 1995) and Geoff Eley / Keith Nield for naturalizing class identity
  • Soviet and Chinese state-Marxist historiography produced political-doctrinal scholarship that cast a long shadow on the broader tradition
  • world-systems analysis (Wallerstein) has been challenged for empirical and theoretical weaknesses (Robert Brenner critique, debates in New Left Review)
  • engagement with non-class identities (gender, race, ethnicity) was historically slow and produced substantial intra-tradition debate
  • the tradition's institutional position has substantially declined since the 1990s in Anglophone academia even as world-economic-history work in Marxist or post-Marxist registers continues.
