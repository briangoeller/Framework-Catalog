Mental Skills Training

Also known as: Sport Psychology Skills

framework · performance · structured-empirical

Sport psychology toolkit covering goal-setting, imagery, self-talk, arousal regulation, and concentration applied to athletic performance.

Mental Skills Training (MST) is the umbrella term in sport psychology for the systematic development of psychological skills supporting athletic performance — principally goal-setting, imagery (visualization), self-talk, arousal regulation, attention control, and performance routines. The framework consolidated through the 1970s-80s sport-psychology research tradition with foundational contributions from Robert Nideffer's Test of Attentional and Interpersonal Style (TAIS, 1976) and attentional-control framework, Terry Orlick's In Pursuit of Excellence (1980, multiple editions) and Mental Skills Workbook tradition, Damon Burton and Daniel Gould's foundational research, and the Association for Applied Sport Psychology (AASP, founded 1986). MST draws methodologically on cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) techniques applied to performance contexts — goal-setting protocols, cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, exposure-based anxiety regulation. The framework has substantial empirical literature, AASP Certified Mental Performance Consultant (CMPC) certification infrastructure, and substantial application across Olympic sport, professional sport, performing arts, and broader high-performance contexts.

Core components

  • Core mental skills typically taught: (1) Goal-setting: SMART goals, outcome/performance/process goal distinction, short-term/long-term goal hierarchies
  • (2) Imagery (visualization): mental rehearsal of skills and competition scenarios using PETTLEP framework (physical, environment, task, timing, learning, emotion, perspective)
  • (3) Self-talk: instructional, motivational, regulatory self-statements
  • cognitive restructuring of negative self-talk
  • (4) Arousal regulation: progressive muscle relaxation, breathing techniques, biofeedback, energizing techniques
  • (5) Attention control: Nideffer's four-quadrant framework (broad-internal, broad-external, narrow-internal, narrow-external)
  • attention-switching practice
  • mindfulness-based attention training
  • (6) Performance routines: pre-performance routines (pre-shot, pre-serve, pre-event), competition-day routines, recovery routines
  • Sport-specific assessment: Test of Attentional and Interpersonal Style (TAIS), Profile of Mood States (POMS), Sport Anxiety Scale, Trait Sport-Confidence Inventory, others
  • Periodization of mental skills (parallel to physical periodization): off-season skill acquisition, pre-season application, in-season maintenance and competition, post-season recovery
  • Integration with physical training: MST typically delivered alongside physical training rather than as standalone intervention
  • Methodological foundation: substantial cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) heritage with sport-performance adaptation
  • Modern extensions: mindfulness-based interventions (MAC, Mindful Sport Performance Enhancement), acceptance-and-commitment therapy (ACT) applications, self-compassion-based approaches, identity-and-values work
  • AASP Certified Mental Performance Consultant (CMPC): credentialing infrastructure

Primary use case

Foundational sport-psychology service delivery framework across competitive sport; applied principally in: Olympic sport (substantial MST integration in national governing-body performance support across all sports), professional team sport (NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL, soccer leagues, rugby), individual sport (tennis, golf, track and field, swimming), performing arts (music performance, dance), high-stakes professional contexts (military special operations, surgery, public speaking, first responders); AASP Certified Mental Performance Consultant (CMPC): substantial credentialing and professional-practice infrastructure; Olympic Performance Service infrastructure (US Olympic and Paralympic Committee, English Institute of Sport, Australian Institute of Sport, Canadian Sport Institute, others) providing MST as core service; academic and professional reference in sport psychology, exercise science, performance psychology, and applied-psychology literature; core teaching in sport-psychology graduate programs and AASP-accredited training programs; complementary to Periodization, Long-Term Athlete Development, Inner Game tradition, and broader high-performance support frameworks.

Common criticisms

  • MST's empirical evidence base has been substantially studied with mixed findings — substantial peer-reviewed literature documents positive effects of specific techniques (goal-setting, imagery, self-talk) on specific outcomes, but effect sizes are typically modest, ecological validity (transfer from controlled studies to actual competition) is limited, and substantial publication-bias concerns have been raised in the broader sport-psychology literature
  • the umbrella-framework status of MST produces methodological-evaluation challenges — different MST programs combine different techniques with different intensity and duration, making comparison across programs and isolation of specific-technique effects difficult
  • the cognitive-behavioral foundations have produced ongoing methodological debate as third-wave approaches (mindfulness, acceptance, self-compassion) challenge traditional CBT-style cognitive-restructuring techniques in performance contexts — Frank Gardner and Zella Moore's Mindfulness-Acceptance-Commitment (MAC) approach explicitly questions self-talk and arousal-regulation techniques' effectiveness
  • the imagery research base specifically has been substantially debated, with PETTLEP framework providing structure but real-world effect-size variation substantial
  • individual-difference variation in technique responsiveness is substantial — what works for one athlete may not work for another, with limited evidence guiding individualization
  • the professional-practice infrastructure (AASP certification, commercial sport-psychology consulting) has been argued by some critics to exceed the empirical-evidence justification, with marketing-driven service adoption outpacing evidence-driven adoption
  • cross-cultural applicability is uneven — most MST research has been conducted with Western (particularly US-American and Anglo-Australian) populations, with limited evidence on applicability across diverse cultural contexts
  • integration with sport-medicine and clinical-psychology services produces overlap concerns where mental-skills coaches may inappropriately address clinical-mental-health concerns (depression, anxiety disorders, eating disorders) that require licensed clinical care
  • recent research on athlete mental-health (Cosh, Crocker, Whittaker) has substantially expanded the field beyond performance-skills-only orientation, with some critics arguing traditional MST has underweighted athlete wellbeing relative to performance metrics.
