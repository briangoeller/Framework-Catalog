Mintzberg's Management Roles

framework · management · organizing-schema

Ten managerial roles grouped as interpersonal, informational, and decisional.

Henry Mintzberg's ten managerial roles framework, presented in The Nature of Managerial Work (1973) based on direct structured observation of five executive managers across one week each, was a sharp empirical corrective to the prior 'classical' picture of management as a rational planning, organizing, commanding, coordinating, and controlling activity (Fayol's POCCC). Mintzberg's observation found managerial work was instead characterized by brevity, variety, fragmentation, action orientation, and oral and informal communication. He grouped the activities into ten roles in three categories: interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), and decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator). The framework remains foundational in management education for its empirical realism about what managers actually do versus what management theory had previously claimed.

Primary sources

Core components

  • Three role categories: Interpersonal (figurehead, leader, liaison), Informational (monitor, disseminator, spokesperson), Decisional (entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator)
  • Empirical observation of actual managerial work
  • Critique of classical Fayol-style management theory
  • Brevity, variety, fragmentation as managerial-work characteristics

Primary use case

Management education and training; empirical foundation for understanding actual managerial work; basis for executive coaching and self-assessment; reference framework in studies of how managers spend their time.

Common criticisms

  • Original sample was five managers — small for broad inferential claims
  • sample was North American executives in the early 1970s, raising generalization questions
  • ten-role categorization has overlap and ambiguous boundaries
  • doesn't distinguish effective from ineffective managers
  • subsequent replications (Kurke and Aldrich 1983, Tengblad 2006) have produced largely confirming but evolved findings
  • technology has substantially changed managerial work since 1973 in ways the framework wasn't designed to capture.
