Nonviolent Communication

Also known as: NVC

framework · psychology · organizing-schema

Marshall Rosenberg's framework structuring communication around observation, feeling, need, and request.

Nonviolent Communication (NVC), also called Compassionate Communication, is the framework developed by American clinical psychologist Marshall Rosenberg from the 1960s onward, structuring communication around four components: observation (specific factual description without evaluation), feeling (emotional state in the body), need (universal human need underlying the feeling), and request (specific actionable proposal). Rosenberg drew on Carl Rogers' person-centered therapy (Rosenberg's doctoral mentor was Rogers), Mahatma Gandhi's nonviolence ethic, broader humanistic psychology, and Rosenberg's experience as a civil-rights-era mediator. The framework was articulated in Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication: A Language of Life (first edition 1999, expanded 2003 and 2015 editions). The Center for Nonviolent Communication (founded 1984) supports a global network of certified trainers. NVC is widely practiced in conflict-resolution, education, parenting, organizational, and prison-rehabilitation contexts, with substantial popular but limited rigorous-empirical validation.

Band notes

Practitioner adoption is substantial but rigorous empirical validation is limited; some critics have noted that the method's self-presentation as 'nonviolent' begs the question.

Core components

  • Four-component communication structure: Observation, Feeling, Need, Request (OFNR)
  • Observation: specific factual description without evaluation, interpretation, or judgment
  • Feeling: emotional state distinguished from thoughts and judgments dressed as feelings
  • Need: universal human need (autonomy, connection, contribution, physical sustenance, integrity, etc.)
  • Request: specific, doable, present-tense, positive-action request (distinguished from demand by acceptance of 'no')
  • Empathic listening: receiving another's expression in OFNR frame
  • Self-empathy: applying OFNR frame to one's own experience
  • Distinction between needs (universal) and strategies (specific ways of meeting needs)
  • Distinction between request and demand (defined by speaker's response to 'no')
  • Giraffe / jackal metaphor (representing NVC vs. evaluative communication)

Primary use case

Communication framework in conflict-resolution, mediation, and restorative-justice contexts; parenting and educational practice (NVC in schools movement); organizational and team-development applications; prison rehabilitation programs (Marshall Rosenberg's substantial work in correctional settings); international conflict and reconciliation work (Rwanda, Israel-Palestine, Bosnia practitioner programs); personal and relationship-development practitioner literature; Center for Nonviolent Communication certification and training infrastructure.

Common criticisms

  • NVC's empirical validation is limited relative to its widespread practitioner adoption — controlled studies are sparse, with Connor and Wentworth 2012, Marlow et al. 2012, and Burleson and Goldsmith 1998 representing the small empirical literature
  • the framework's self-presentation as 'nonviolent' has been argued by critics including Robert Wachbroit and others to be question-begging — anyone disagreeing with NVC framing is implicitly characterized as 'violent' or 'jackal,' producing rhetorical asymmetry
  • the prescribed grammar (specific syntactic forms for each of the four components) can become rigid and artificial in practice, with critics noting that skilled NVC speakers sound stilted or therapy-speak in contexts where ordinary language would communicate more effectively
  • the framework's emphasis on universal needs has been challenged on cross-cultural and political grounds — what counts as a 'need' versus a 'preference' or 'cultural expectation' is not value-neutral, and the framework's individualist needs-language may not translate well to collectivist or non-Western communicative cultures
  • ambiguity about whether NVC is a communication skill, a therapeutic intervention, a worldview, or a moral commitment produces difficulty in evaluating its effectiveness against specific claims
  • movement-internal disputes about Rosenberg's institutional legacy and the Center for Nonviolent Communication's positions have produced some practitioner splits
  • the framework's inattention to power asymmetries (a harassed worker and their harasser are both invited to speak in OFNR form) has been argued by feminist and anti-oppression critics to underweight structural injustices
  • the workshop and training commercial infrastructure raises ordinary concerns about institutional incentives shaping framework development.
