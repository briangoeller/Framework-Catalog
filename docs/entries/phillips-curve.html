Phillips Curve

framework · economics · structured-empirical

Empirical regularity (and theoretical debate) around inverse relation of unemployment and inflation.

The Phillips Curve began as A.W. Phillips's 1958 documentation of an inverse empirical relationship between unemployment and the rate of change of nominal wages in UK data from 1861-1957, which Samuelson and Solow translated to a US context as a stable inflation-unemployment trade-off available for policy exploitation. Friedman and Phelps independently argued in the late 1960s that any such trade-off was a short-run phenomenon driven by inflation expectations errors, and that the long-run Phillips curve was vertical at the natural rate of unemployment — a prediction substantially vindicated by 1970s stagflation. The expectations-augmented Phillips Curve became the workhorse of monetary policy thinking through inflation-targeting regimes, but the apparent flattening of the empirical curve from the 1990s onward (particularly through the 2010s expansion) and its sudden steepening in 2021-2022 have produced a substantial ongoing debate about the framework's reliability for policy.

Originators

A.W. Phillips (original empirical observation); Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow (US application); Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps (expectations-augmented version); Robert Lucas (Lucas critique) high

Year / Decade

1958 (Phillips); 1960 (Samuelson-Solow US extension); 1968 (Friedman-Phelps natural-rate critique) high

Primary sources

Phillips, A.W. (1958). 'The Relation between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861-1957', Economica, Friedman, M. (1968). 'The Role of Monetary Policy', American Economic Review high

Core components

  • Empirical inverse relationship between unemployment and wage/price inflation
  • Short-run vs long-run distinction
  • Expectations augmentation
  • Natural Rate of Unemployment / NAIRU
  • Vertical long-run curve
  • Lucas critique on policy-regime invariance
  • Recent flattening debate

Primary use case

Monetary policy formulation; central bank decision-making framework; inflation-unemployment tradeoff communication; framework for dual-mandate central banks (e.g., US Fed) balancing employment and price stability.

Common criticisms

  • Stagflation in the 1970s demolished the original stable-tradeoff reading
  • Lucas critique applies to estimated curves used for policy because the curve itself depends on the policy regime
  • observed flattening in 1990s-2010s raised questions about NAIRU's stability and meaning
  • sudden 2021-2022 steepening surprised forecasters and renewed methodological debates
  • difficulty distinguishing supply from demand contributions to observed inflation-unemployment combinations.
