Porter's Value Chain

framework · management · organizing-schema

Decomposes the firm into primary and support activities to locate sources of value.

Porter's Value Chain, introduced in Competitive Advantage (1985), decomposes a firm's operations into a sequence of primary activities — inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service — and four support activities — firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement — that together generate the firm's margin. The framework's analytical purpose is to locate the specific activities that create or could create competitive advantage through cost reduction or differentiation, and to expose the linkages and dependencies between them. The Value Chain pairs with Porter's Five Forces (industry-level analysis) and Generic Strategies (positioning choice) to form an integrated strategic toolkit, and has been substantially extended into supply-chain analysis and the broader 'value system' connecting suppliers, the firm, channels, and buyers.

Originators

Michael E. Porter high

Year / Decade

1985 (Competitive Advantage) high

Primary sources

Porter, M.E. (1985). Competitive Advantage: Creating and Sustaining Superior Performance high

Core components

  • Primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service)
  • Support activities (firm infrastructure, HRM, technology development, procurement)
  • Margin
  • Linkages between activities
  • Value system (suppliers, firm, channels, buyers)
  • Cost drivers and uniqueness drivers

Primary use case

Identifying sources of competitive advantage at the activity level; cost analysis and differentiation analysis; basis for activity-based costing and supply-chain analysis; structuring strategic audits.

Common criticisms

  • Activity categories were tailored to manufacturing and translate awkwardly to service, software, and platform businesses
  • treats the firm's boundaries as given and stable
  • underweights intangibles (brand, knowledge, relationships) increasingly central in modern competition
  • assumes linear value flow that doesn't fit cyclical or networked production
  • static rather than dynamic across time.
