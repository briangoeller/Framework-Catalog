Positive Psychology

Also known as: PERMA

framework · psychology · structured-empirical

Seligman's flourishing framework: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment.

Positive Psychology was launched as an organized scientific movement by Martin Seligman during his 1998 American Psychological Association presidency, with the explicit aim of redirecting psychological research and practice from its predominant focus on pathology toward scientific understanding of well-being, character strengths, and the conditions that produce human flourishing. Foundational figures alongside Seligman include Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (flow), Christopher Peterson (character strengths), Barbara Fredrickson (broaden-and-build), Ed Diener (subjective well-being), and Sonja Lyubomirsky (happiness interventions). The PERMA model, articulated in Seligman's Flourish (2011), identifies five elements of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment. Adjacent frameworks include the VIA Classification of 24 character strengths and Seligman's earlier authentic-happiness model. Positive Psychology has produced a substantial intervention literature and influenced education, workplace mental health, and therapy, though core claims have faced replication challenges in the broader social-psychology replication crisis.

Core components

  • PERMA: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment
  • VIA Classification of 24 character strengths under 6 virtues
  • Subjective Well-Being (SWB) tradition (Diener)
  • Flow as central to Engagement
  • Distinction between hedonic (pleasure) and eudaimonic (meaning) well-being
  • Positive interventions (gratitude practices, three good things, signature strengths use)
  • Authentic happiness model (precursor)

Primary use case

Foundation of well-being research and intervention; school-based positive education programs (Geelong Grammar, Penn Resilience); workplace well-being initiatives; clinical complement to traditional pathology-focused treatment; foundation for life and executive coaching; framework for thinking about flourishing in public-policy and organizational contexts.

Common criticisms

  • Several signature studies (some happiness interventions, certain positive-emotion findings) have struggled to replicate in the broader social-psychology replication crisis
  • critics including Barbara Held and Ronald Miller argue the movement undervalues the genuinely useful role of negative emotion and pathologizes appropriate distress
  • intervention effect sizes are typically modest and decay over time
  • cultural specificity of 'flourishing' frame raises cross-cultural concerns
  • commercial coaching and self-help adjacent to Positive Psychology often substantially overclaim what the science supports
  • military-focused applications (Comprehensive Soldier Fitness) have drawn ethical and efficacy critiques
  • PERMA's specific five-factor structure is one of several competing well-being frameworks (e.g., Ryff's six dimensions).
