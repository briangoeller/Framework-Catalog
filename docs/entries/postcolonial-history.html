Postcolonial History

framework · history · philosophical-tradition

Historical approach critiquing colonial frameworks and recovering perspectives marginalized by colonial-era historiography.

Postcolonial History is the historiographical approach critiquing colonial-era historical frameworks, recovering perspectives marginalized by colonial historiography, and analyzing the continuing effects of colonial knowledge structures on historical writing. The approach is associated foundationally with Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), which argued that European scholarly representations of 'the Orient' were inseparable from colonial power relations and produced systematic distortions of Asian and Middle Eastern history; subsequent foundational contributions include Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' (1988), Homi Bhabha's The Location of Culture (1994), and Dipesh Chakrabarty's Provincializing Europe (2000). Postcolonial History draws on Marxist historiography, poststructuralist theory, and the Subaltern Studies tradition (closely related but institutionally and methodologically distinct), and has substantially transformed African, South Asian, Middle Eastern, and Latin American historiography while also reshaping European and North American historiography of empire.

Core components

  • Critique of colonial-era historical frameworks: orientalism, civilizational hierarchy, modernization theory
  • Recovery of perspectives marginalized by colonial historiography (indigenous, peasant, female, low-caste)
  • Analysis of colonial knowledge structures: the production of Orientalist, Africanist, and other colonial-academic disciplines
  • Poststructuralist methodological commitments: discourse analysis, deconstruction of binary oppositions (colonizer/colonized, civilized/primitive)
  • Engagement with continuing colonial effects: neocolonialism, intellectual decolonization, decolonization of curriculum
  • Hybridity, mimicry, ambivalence as analytical concepts (Bhabha)
  • Provincialization of Europe: refusing to treat European historical experience as universal-template (Chakrabarty)
  • Methodological connections to Subaltern Studies, History from Below, Marxist historiography

Primary use case

Foundational approach for African, South Asian, Middle Eastern, Caribbean, and Latin American historiography; research framework for history of empire, colonialism, slavery, and decolonization; intellectual foundation for decolonial studies, indigenous studies, and global South history; academic reference in cultural studies, comparative literature, and anthropology; pedagogical canon in postcolonial-historiography graduate curricula; engagement with contemporary debates about decolonization of curriculum, reparative history, and scholarly politics.

Common criticisms

  • Postcolonial History has been substantially contested both within and outside the tradition — Said's Orientalism (1978) was substantially critiqued by Bernard Lewis ('The Question of Orientalism' 1982), Robert Irwin (For Lust of Knowing 2006), and Aijaz Ahmad (In Theory 1992) on empirical and methodological grounds, with Ahmad's Marxist critique from the left arguing Said's framework neglects class structures and material conditions
  • Spivak's 'Can the Subaltern Speak?' has been substantially debated within the school itself, with disputes about what the essay actually argues and what political implications follow
  • Vivek Chibber's Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital (2013) provided a substantial Marxist critique of postcolonial theory's treatment of capitalism, generating substantial subsequent debate
  • the field's poststructuralist methodological commitments produce tension with empirical-historical scholarship, with some critics arguing postcolonial history privileges theoretical sophistication over evidentiary discipline
  • the field's institutional position is predominantly in elite Anglophone academia, raising questions about whose decolonization is being articulated
  • gender blindness in early postcolonial work was substantially documented by feminist scholars (Chandra Talpade Mohanty 'Under Western Eyes' 1984), prompting both critique and subsequent incorporation
  • the relationship between academic postcolonial history and material decolonization politics is ongoing debate
  • reception in the global South has produced its own critiques arguing postcolonial theory remains substantially Western-academic (Hamid Dabashi, Walter Mignolo's decolonial alternative).
