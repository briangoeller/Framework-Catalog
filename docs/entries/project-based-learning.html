Project-Based Learning

Also known as: PBL

framework · education · organizing-schema

Extended student inquiry organized around authentic projects.

Project-Based Learning is the pedagogical approach in which students learn through extended engagement with authentic projects that culminate in publicly-presented products. The framework descends from John Dewey's progressive education tradition and William Heard Kilpatrick's 1918 'The Project Method' (Teachers College Record), with substantial contemporary development through the Buck Institute for Education (now PBLWorks), High Tech High, and other organizations. Modern Project-Based Learning is structured around a Driving Question, sustained inquiry over multiple weeks, authenticity (real-world relevance), student voice and choice, reflection, critique and revision, and a public product. PBLWorks's 'Gold Standard PBL' framework articulates seven essential design elements that distinguish PBL from less rigorous project-based activities. PBL has substantial adoption in K-12 STEM education and in some alternative-school models (notably High Tech High); empirical evidence on outcomes is mixed and substantially dependent on implementation quality, with meta-analyses (Chen & Yang 2019; Condliffe 2017) showing modest positive effects on average with substantial variation. The framework is commonly confused with Problem-Based Learning (different lineage, originally medical-education focused) and with project-based activities that don't meet PBL design standards.

  • Critique and revision
  • Public product

  • Distinction from Problem-Based Learning (different lineage)

Primary use case

K-12 STEM education particularly; alternative-school and project-school models (High Tech High and similar); high-school career and technical education; integration with maker-space and design-thinking curricula; basis for some district and school-network instructional models; teacher professional development through PBLWorks and similar organizations.

Common criticisms

  • Implementation is genuinely difficult — Gold Standard PBL requires substantial teacher expertise, time, and resources that many schools don't have
  • many 'PBL' implementations are project-based activities that don't meet design standards
  • meta-analytic effect sizes are modest and variable
  • potential equity concerns when project authenticity advantages students with relevant out-of-school resources and experiences
  • tension with content-coverage demands and standardized-test accountability
  • commercial certification and curriculum industry has produced compliance-style adoption
  • assessment of project outcomes is harder than traditional tests, raising grading consistency concerns
  • works better in some disciplines (science, social studies) than others (foundational mathematics, world languages)
  • confusion with Problem-Based Learning is widespread.
