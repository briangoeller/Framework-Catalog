Raga System

framework · music theory · philosophical-tradition

Indian classical framework organizing melodic material through ragas characterized by ascending and descending scales, characteristic phrases, and affective and time-of-day associations.

The Raga System is the foundational pitch-organizing framework of Indian classical music, in which a raga is not merely a scale but a substantively complete framework specifying ascending and descending melodic forms (arohana, avarohana), characteristic phrases (pakad, sancara), permitted pitch ornaments (gamaka, meend), affective associations (rasa), performance time-of-day or season, and characteristic compositional and improvisational practices. The system has ancient Indian theoretical antecedents in Bharata's Nāṭyaśāstra (variously dated 200 BCE–200 CE) and Śārṅgadeva's Saṅgītaratnākara (13th century), with substantial subsequent development through the medieval and early-modern periods producing distinct Hindustani (North Indian) and Carnatic (South Indian) traditions. Hindustani raga organization was systematized by Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande (early 20th century) into the ten-thaat system; Carnatic music uses the 72-melakarta system codified by Venkatamakhin (Caturdaṇḍīprakāśikā, 1660s). Transmission is principally oral through the guru-shishya parampara and the gharana lineage system.

Originators

Bharata Muni (Nāṭyaśāstra c.200 BCE-200 CE, foundational treatise on music theory and performance); Śārṅgadeva (Saṅgītaratnākara 13th century, comprehensive medieval codification); Venkatamakhin (Caturdaṇḍīprakāśikā 1660s, Carnatic 72-melakarta system); Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande (early 20th century, Hindustani ten-thaat systematization); Vishnu Digambar Paluskar (parallel early-20th-century reform tradition); Pandit Omkarnath Thakur and successive 20th-century theorists high

Year / Decade

Ancient antecedents (Sāmaveda, Nāṭyaśāstra c.200 BCE-200 CE); 13th c (Saṅgītaratnākara); 17th c (Carnatic 72 melakarta); early 20th c (Bhatkhande) high

Primary sources

Bharata. Nāṭyaśāstra (c.200 BCE-200 CE, foundational treatise), Śārṅgadeva (13th century). Saṅgītaratnākara, Bhatkhande, V.N. (1910-1932). Hindustani Sangeet Paddhati, Rowell, L. (1992). Music and Musical Thought in Early India high

Core components

  • Raga: melodic-mode framework substantially more elaborated than 'scale' — encompassing pitch material, characteristic phrases, ornaments, affective and seasonal associations, performance practice
  • Ārohaṇa and avarohaṇa (ascending and descending pitch sequences, often asymmetric)
  • Pakaḍ and sañcāra (characteristic identifying melodic phrases)
  • Gamaka and meend (ornament systems with substantial regional variation)
  • Vādī and saṃvādī (most-prominent and second-most-prominent pitches)
  • Time-of-day and seasonal raga-performance associations (samaya)
  • Hindustani thaat system: ten parent scales (Bilawal, Khamaj, Bhairav, Asavari, Kafi, Bhairavi, Kalyan, Marwa, Poorvi, Todi)
  • Carnatic 72-melakarta system: comprehensive sampurna-raga taxonomy
  • Janya raga: derivative ragas not in melakarta/thaat systems
  • Guru-shishya parampara (oral transmission) and gharana (lineage-school) systems

Primary use case

Foundational pitch framework for Hindustani and Carnatic classical music performance and composition; compositional and improvisational reference for vocal genres (khyāl, thumri, dhrupad in Hindustani; kṛti, varṇam, rāgam-tānam-pallavi in Carnatic) and instrumental traditions; pedagogical foundation for Indian classical training through guru-shishya and institutional (gharana, university) systems; research framework in Indian musicology and ethnomusicology; increasing cross-cultural reference in fusion and global musical practice.

Common criticisms

  • Bhatkhande's ten-thaat systematization of Hindustani music has been substantially critiqued by scholars including V.N. Patwardhan, Vamanrao Deshpande, and contemporary musicologists for imposing a Western-influenced scale-classification framework on a tradition whose practical organization is more nuanced — many actually-performed ragas fit awkwardly into the thaat scheme, and the system can obscure substantively important differences between ragas sharing a thaat
  • the gharana lineage system preserves substantial musical knowledge but is also a site of intra-tradition debate about authenticity, transmission accuracy, and whose practice is canonical (recent scholarship by Daniel Neuman, Bonnie Wade, James Kippen documents this)
  • the Anglophone reception of Indian classical music since the 1960s has produced popular-tradition simplifications that elide the distinction between raga as practical tradition and raga as exoticist Western consumption category
  • Carnatic-Hindustani relationship is itself contested — they share ancestral theoretical antecedents but have substantially distinct practical traditions, and treating them as variants of one 'Indian classical' tradition is sometimes critiqued as nationalist projection
  • the textbook claim of unbroken ancient continuity has been complicated by recent historiography (David Trasoff, Lewis Rowell) showing significant historical breaks and reformulations
  • transcription of raga into Western staff notation produces substantial information loss particularly for ornaments, microtonal nuances, and melodic-phrase identity.
