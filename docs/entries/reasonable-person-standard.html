Reasonable Person Standard

framework · law · doctrinal-institutional

Hypothetical ordinarily prudent person used as a benchmark for negligence.

The Reasonable Person Standard is the foundational benchmark of common-law tort negligence — the hypothetical 'ordinarily prudent person' against whose conduct the defendant's actual conduct is measured to determine whether the defendant exercised due care. The standard descends from English common-law origins, with Vaughan v. Menlove (1837, English Court of Common Pleas) often cited as foundational — Tindal CJ articulating that the standard isn't what the defendant himself thought reasonable but 'such a degree of prudence as would be exercised by a man of ordinary prudence under like circumstances.' The standard's attractions include: providing objective benchmark independent of defendant's actual capabilities or beliefs; allowing flexible application across diverse circumstances; embodying community expectations of ordinary care. The standard has been substantially developed through subsequent jurisprudence, including specifications for: the reasonable person with the defendant's physical characteristics (the blind person, the child); the reasonable professional (heightened standard for those with special skill, particularly medical and legal professionals); the reasonable person of ordinary prudence in particular contexts (the reasonable driver, the reasonable property owner). Substantial criticism has emerged about whose 'reasonable person' the standard reflects — feminist legal theorists including Patricia Cain, Leslie Bender argued through the 1980s-90s that the reasonable person was implicitly gendered male, with the 'reasonable person' standard sometimes inadequate to address sexual harassment, domestic violence, and other gendered harms (the reasonable woman standard developed in some sexual harassment jurisprudence — Ellison v. Brady 1991, 9th Circuit). Cross-cultural variation, racial bias in juror application, and the standard's tension with substantial individual variation in physical and cognitive capacities all complicate the standard's practical application.

Core components

  • Hypothetical 'ordinarily prudent person' as benchmark
  • Objective rather than subjective standard
  • Application to negligence determination
  • Connection to but distinction from defendant's actual conduct
  • Variations: reasonable person with defendant's physical characteristics, reasonable professional, reasonable person in specific contexts
  • Reasonable woman standard in some sexual-harassment jurisprudence (Ellison v. Brady 1991)
  • Connection to Hand Formula as economic analysis variant
  • Foundation of common-law tort negligence
  • Substantial doctrinal flexibility

Primary use case

Foundation of common-law tort negligence jurisprudence; reference framework in essentially every common-law tort system globally (US, UK, Canada, Australia, India, etc.); basis for substantial personal-injury, product-liability, professional-malpractice litigation; pedagogical foundation in law school tort curricula; integration with broader liability framework; foundation for many regulatory standards adapting reasonable-care concepts; influence on insurance and risk-management practice.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial feminist legal critique that the 'reasonable person' has been implicitly gendered male — Patricia Cain, Leslie Bender, others argued through 1980s-90s that the standard reflected particular male perspectives, especially in sexual harassment, domestic violence, and rape jurisprudence
  • the reasonable woman standard developed in some sexual harassment jurisprudence (Ellison v. Brady 1991, 9th Circuit) addressed some of these concerns
  • racial bias in juror application of 'reasonable person' standards is substantially documented
  • cross-cultural variation in what counts as reasonable creates tension with universal-standard claim
  • the standard's flexibility (claimed strength) is also weakness — provides little determinate guidance ex ante
  • tension between objective 'reasonable person' and substantive accommodations for defendant characteristics (children, physical disabilities)
  • commercial liability industry has produced substantial litigation over what 'reasonable' means in particular contexts
  • integration with quantitative cost-benefit analysis (Hand Formula) raises questions about whether the standard is intuitive moral judgment or implicit economic analysis
  • tendency for 'reasonable person' to embed implicit class, race, and cultural assumptions about what is normal and prudent.
