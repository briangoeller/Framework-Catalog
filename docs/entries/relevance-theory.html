Relevance Theory

framework · linguistics · organizing-schema

Sperber and Wilson's pragmatic framework: communication guided by relevance maximization.

Relevance Theory is the pragmatic framework articulated by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson in their 1986 Relevance: Communication and Cognition (revised 1995 edition substantially extending the framework), proposing that human communication is fundamentally guided by a single principle of relevance rather than by multiple Gricean maxims (Cooperative Principle, separately enriched). The framework's central commitments: (1) cognition tends toward relevance maximization — humans automatically attend to information they expect to yield greatest cognitive effects (true, plausible, useful information) for least processing effort; (2) communication exploits this cognitive tendency — speakers signal that their utterances are 'relevant enough' to be worth audience attention, and audiences interpret utterances under the assumption they will yield relevance benefits proportional to processing costs; (3) the Communicative Principle of Relevance — every act of ostensive communication conveys a presumption of its own optimal relevance; (4) inference rather than encoding-decoding — communication is fundamentally inferential, with hearers reconstructing speaker meaning through relevance-driven inference rather than decoding fixed linguistic-meaning packets. The framework substantially modified Gricean pragmatics: where Grice posited multiple maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner), Sperber-Wilson argue a single relevance principle suffices and explains both cooperation and apparent maxim violation; the framework integrates communication-pragmatic analysis with cognitive science (drawing on Fodor's modularity of mind and broader cognitive science). Relevance Theory has substantial empirical implementation including work on metaphor (relevance-driven inference rather than CMT's mapping framework), irony (echoic interpretation accounts), implicit communication, and substantial second-language acquisition applications. Critics including neo-Griceans (Levinson, Horn) argue that relevance theory's single-principle parsimony obscures genuine distinctions Grice identified, and that 'relevance' as theoretical primitive is operationally vague.

Core components

  • Cognitive Principle of Relevance (cognition tends toward relevance maximization)
  • Communicative Principle of Relevance (ostensive communication conveys presumption of optimal relevance)
  • Cognitive effects vs processing effort tradeoff
  • Inference rather than encoding-decoding
  • Single-principle alternative to Gricean maxims
  • Application to metaphor, irony, implicit communication
  • Connection to broader cognitive science (Fodor modularity)
  • Distinction from neo-Gricean accounts (Horn, Levinson)
  • Substantial second-language acquisition application

Primary use case

Major framework in pragmatics; basis for substantial work on inferential communication, metaphor, irony, implicit meaning; reference framework in cognitive pragmatics education; foundation for substantial cross-disciplinary work on communication; integration with cognitive science; pedagogical foundation in advanced linguistic pragmatics curricula; influence on second-language acquisition research; foundation for some computational linguistics work on inference; substantial application in literary stylistics and discourse analysis.

Common criticisms

  • Single-principle parsimony has been criticized by neo-Griceans (Levinson, Horn) as obscuring genuine distinctions Grice identified — different maxims (quantity, quality, manner) capture different communicative phenomena that 'relevance' as single principle flattens
  • 'relevance' as theoretical primitive is operationally vague — what counts as 'relevant enough' or 'optimally relevant' involves substantial interpretive judgment
  • the cognitive-effects vs processing-effort tradeoff is conceptually clean but operationally varied — empirical measurement of these dimensions involves substantial methodological challenges
  • integration with formal semantics and broader linguistic theory has been productive but contested
  • tendency in some applications to invoke 'relevance' decoratively without substantive analysis
  • cross-cultural variation in relevance expectations is substantial — different cultures have different communicative norms about informativeness, indirectness, and other dimensions
  • commercial NLP and AI dialogue systems have invoked relevance theory with varying analytical fidelity
  • debate with neo-Gricean accounts has produced substantial scholarly contribution but also conceptual proliferation that pragmatics-as-discipline has had to navigate
  • the framework's claim to integrate pragmatics with cognitive science has produced productive work but also raises questions about which empirical commitments depend on which theoretical assumptions.
