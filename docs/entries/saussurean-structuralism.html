Saussurean Structuralism

framework · linguistics · organizing-schema

Saussure's framework of language as system of differences with signified and signifier.

Saussurean Structuralism is the linguistic and broader semiotic framework articulated by Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure in his lectures at the University of Geneva (1907-1911), published posthumously as Course in General Linguistics (Cours de linguistique générale, 1916, compiled by students Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye from lecture notes). The framework's central commitments include: (1) the linguistic sign as union of signifier (sound-image, signifiant) and signified (concept, signifié), with the relationship between them being arbitrary (no inherent connection between sound and meaning); (2) language as system of differences — words have meaning through their relationships and contrasts with other words rather than through positive content ('in language there are only differences without positive terms'); (3) distinction between langue (the systematic structure of language as social institution) and parole (individual speech acts); (4) synchronic vs diachronic analysis (language at a moment vs language across time, with synchronic analysis given methodological priority); (5) syntagmatic relations (combinatorial, sequential) vs paradigmatic relations (substitutional, associative). Saussure's framework substantially shaped 20th-century linguistics, anthropology (Lévi-Strauss's structural anthropology), psychoanalysis (Lacan's structural reading of Freud), literary theory (Roland Barthes's early structuralism), and broader semiotic and cultural-theory traditions. The framework provided foundational vocabulary and analytical tools for what became 'structuralism' as broader 20th-century intellectual movement. Subsequent linguistics has substantially complicated Saussure's specific commitments — generative linguistics rejects much of structuralist framework, post-structuralism (Derrida, Foucault, others) substantially critiqued structuralist confidence about systematic meaning, and contemporary cognitive linguistics treats the sign-as-arbitrary claim more contextually than the original framework.

Core components

  • Sign as signifier-signified union (arbitrary connection)
  • Language as system of differences ('only differences without positive terms')
  • Langue (systematic structure) vs parole (individual speech)
  • Synchronic vs diachronic analysis
  • Syntagmatic vs paradigmatic relations
  • Foundation for structural linguistics
  • Subsequent extension to anthropology (Lévi-Strauss), psychoanalysis (Lacan), literary theory (early Barthes)
  • Connection to broader 20th-century structuralism
  • Distinction from later post-structuralism and cognitive linguistics

Primary use case

Foundational framework in 20th-century linguistics; basis for substantial structuralist tradition across disciplines; reference framework in linguistics, semiotics, cultural theory education; foundation for substantial anthropological, psychoanalytic, and literary-theoretical work; integration with broader humanities and social-science traditions; pedagogical foundation in introductory linguistics and humanities curricula; influence on early 20th-century philosophy of language and mind.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial subsequent linguistic work has complicated Saussure's specific commitments — generative linguistics (Universal Grammar, separately enriched) rejects much of structuralist framework, treating language as biological cognitive endowment rather than purely social system of differences
  • post-structuralism (Derrida's Of Grammatology 1967, Foucault, others) substantially critiqued structuralist confidence about systematic meaning, arguing language is more contingent and unstable than Saussurean framework suggests
  • the arbitrariness-of-the-sign claim has been substantially refined in contemporary linguistics — sound symbolism, iconicity in sign languages, and other phenomena suggest substantial non-arbitrary aspects of linguistic signs
  • cognitive linguistics treats meaning more contextually and embodied than Saussurean framework's emphasis on systematic differences
  • the synchronic/diachronic distinction's methodological priority on synchronic analysis has been substantially relaxed in contemporary historical and sociolinguistic work
  • commercial humanities education sometimes presents Saussure as 'the' linguistic theory without engaging substantial subsequent developments
  • cross-cultural application of European-structuralist framings has been complicated by substantial typological work
  • the framework's posthumous compilation from student notes raises questions about which Saussurean commitments are actually his vs editorial reconstruction.
