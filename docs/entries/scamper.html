SCAMPER

technique · management · organizing-schema

Idea-generation prompts: Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to other use, Eliminate, Reverse.

SCAMPER is a checklist-based ideation technique that prompts systematic exploration of an existing product, service, or process by asking what could be Substituted, Combined, Adapted, Modified (or Magnified/Minimized), Put to other use, Eliminated, or Reversed (or Rearranged). The mnemonic was coined by Bob Eberle in his 1971 book Scamper: Games for Imagination Development as an adaptation of Alex Osborn's earlier Idea-Spurring Questions checklist from Applied Imagination (1953), which had been the foundational text in the Creative Problem Solving (CPS) movement. SCAMPER's strength is forcing systematic coverage of a wide range of transformation operations rather than relying on free-association brainstorming. The technique is widely used in design, product development, and creativity training as a structured complement to free-form ideation.

Originators

Bob Eberle (mnemonic); Alex Osborn (Idea-Spurring Questions precursor) high

Year / Decade

1971 (Eberle book); 1953 (Osborn precursor) high

Primary sources

Eberle, R.F. (1971). Scamper: Games for Imagination Development, Osborn, A.F. (1953). Applied Imagination high

Core components

  • S — Substitute
  • C — Combine
  • A — Adapt
  • M — Modify (or Magnify/Minimize)
  • P — Put to other use
  • E — Eliminate
  • R — Reverse (or Rearrange)
  • Systematic checklist application
  • Generative rather than evaluative
  • Adapted from Osborn's Idea-Spurring Questions

Primary use case

Product and service design ideation; creativity training; structured brainstorming for incremental innovation; teaching tool in elementary and corporate creativity programs.

Common criticisms

  • Checklist application can produce volume of ideas without quality discrimination
  • biased toward incremental modification rather than radical reconception
  • categories overlap (Adapt and Modify, Combine and Rearrange)
  • works better for tangible product modifications than for service or strategy ideation
  • doesn't address evaluation or selection of generated ideas
  • can become formulaic and lose generative power with repeated use on the same problem
  • less useful when the underlying problem is poorly defined.
