Show Don't Tell ⚑

technique · narrative studies · organizing-schema

Craft principle preferring scene and sensory detail over exposition.

Show Don't Tell is the foundational creative-writing principle preferring concrete dramatization (scene with sensory detail, action, dialogue) over abstract exposition (telling the reader what to think or feel). The principle's foundational articulation is typically attributed to Anton Chekhov's letter to his brother Alexander (1886): 'Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass' (the wording is substantially apocryphal — the actual Chekhov letter says something similar but the famous phrasing is later paraphrase) and to Henry James's substantial early-20th-century criticism arguing for dramatized presentation over narrative summary. The principle was substantially codified through 20th-century creative-writing pedagogy, particularly in the substantial American creative-writing program tradition that emerged from the Iowa Writers' Workshop (founded 1936) and proliferated globally. The principle's central commitment: rather than narrating that 'John was angry' (telling), show John's anger through his actions, words, expressions, and the sensory details of the scene (showing) — letting readers experience the emotion through dramatic presentation rather than receiving authorial assertion. Show Don't Tell substantially shapes contemporary creative writing across fiction, screenwriting, journalism, memoir, and increasingly business storytelling. Critics including substantial creative-writing teachers note that the principle is too absolute as commonly stated — many successful narratives benefit from substantial telling for pace, exposition, summary, stylistic effect, voice, and reader-narrator intimacy; the contemporary 'show don't tell' rule sometimes produces overwritten descriptive prose with limited narrative drive; experienced writers know when to show and when to tell, with the rigid rule sometimes producing mechanical writing in unsophisticated hands. Substantial recent creative-writing pedagogy has shifted toward 'show and tell' or context-dependent application of the principle.

Common criticisms

  • The principle is too absolute as commonly stated — many successful narratives benefit from substantial telling for pace, exposition, summary, stylistic effect, voice, and reader-narrator intimacy
  • contemporary 'show don't tell' application sometimes produces overwritten descriptive prose with limited narrative drive — paragraphs of sensory detail describing what could be efficiently summarized
  • experienced writers know when to show and when to tell, with the rigid rule sometimes producing mechanical writing in unsophisticated hands
  • commercial creative-writing pedagogy has substantial financial incentive to perpetuate workshop principles that may not always serve writers' substantive development
  • cross-genre application varies — works better for short story and contemporary literary fiction than for genres benefiting from authorial voice (essay, memoir, satirical fiction, much science fiction and fantasy)
  • the Chekhov attribution is substantially apocryphal, complicating the principle's authoritative-source claim
  • integration with reading-experience research is limited — substantial empirical work on reader processing suggests showing isn't always more effective than telling
  • cross-cultural application of European-American workshop framings has uneven results
  • tendency for show-don't-tell training to produce graduates who can render scenes vividly but struggle with structural narrative challenges
  • recent literary fiction (Rachel Cusk, Karl Ove Knausgård, Sheila Heti, others) has explicitly returned to substantial telling/exposition modes that contradict the rigid principle.
