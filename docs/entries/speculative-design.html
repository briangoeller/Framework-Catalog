Speculative Design

Also known as: Critical Design

framework · design · organizing-schema

Design used to provoke debate about possible futures rather than to solve problems.

Speculative Design (also Critical Design, Design Fiction, Discursive Design) is the design practice using designed artifacts, scenarios, and provocations to explore possible, probable, and preferable futures — opening conversations about what we want and don't want from emerging technologies, social arrangements, and systems — rather than to solve immediate problems through design. The discipline was substantially developed by Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby at the Royal College of Art (Dunne's Hertzian Tales 1999, Dunne and Raby's Design Noir 2001 and Speculative Everything 2013), with parallel development through Bruce Sterling's 'design fiction' (2005) and Julian Bleecker's 'design fiction: A short essay on design, science, fact and fiction' (2009). The framework's central commitments include: design as provocation rather than problem-solving; artifacts as 'props for the unimaginable' (Dunne-Raby) — physical objects, scenarios, films, exhibitions that make possible futures tangible and discussable; explicitly value-laden and normative rather than neutral or service-oriented; substantial overlap with art practice while remaining distinctively design-oriented through emphasis on plausibility, materiality, and use-context. Famous examples include Dunne-Raby's 'Designs for an Overpopulated Planet' (2009), Superflux's 'United Micro Kingdoms' (2013), various design fiction projects exploring AI ethics, surveillance, climate futures, and biotechnology consequences. The framework has been substantially influential in design education (most graduate design programs now include speculative-design coursework), in technology criticism (where designers create artifacts illustrating concerns about emerging technologies), and increasingly in policy and futures-thinking contexts. Critics argue that speculative design can be self-indulgent, politically vague, or commercially co-opted (companies adopting 'speculative' framing for product marketing), with Cameron Tonkinwise's 'Just Design' (2015) providing one substantial critique.

Core components

  • Design as provocation rather than problem-solving
  • Possible, Probable, Preferable futures (cone-of-futures from futures studies)
  • Designed artifacts as 'props for the unimaginable'
  • Substantial use of physical artifacts, scenarios, films, exhibitions
  • Critical engagement with emerging technologies and social arrangements
  • Distinction from problem-solving design
  • Overlap with art practice while remaining design-oriented
  • Connection to futures studies and foresight
  • Foundation for design fiction in technology criticism
  • Substantial influence on design education

Primary use case

Critical engagement with emerging technologies in design education and practice; technology criticism and ethics work in design (substantial work on AI ethics, surveillance, biotechnology, climate futures); foundation for some policy futures-thinking processes; reference framework in design research; basis for substantial museum exhibition work (V&A London, MoMA NY, others); integration with broader futures studies and foresight; pedagogical foundation in graduate design programs; influence on technology journalism and public-interest design.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial published critique: Cameron Tonkinwise's 'Just Design' (2015) argued speculative design has been substantially apolitical and self-indulgent, producing artifacts that appear critical but rarely lead to substantive political action
  • Pedro Oliveira and Luiza Prado's critique that speculative design is substantially Western-European and white-male-dominated, with 'futures' that reflect particular cultural commitments elevated to universal
  • commercial co-option as companies adopt 'speculative' framing for product marketing without substantive critical engagement
  • tendency to produce provocative artifacts without sustained engagement with affected communities
  • integration with substantive futures-studies methodology is often weak
  • the framework's emphasis on 'making the unimaginable tangible' can produce shock-value work that doesn't drive substantive understanding
  • cross-cultural application of European-design-school framings has been substantially contested
  • tension between speculative design's institutional success (museum exhibitions, design schools) and its critical aspirations (challenging dominant systems)
  • some applications conflate speculative design with science fiction or general futures thinking without engaging the framework's specific commitments
  • works better as provocation than as practical futures-decision input.
