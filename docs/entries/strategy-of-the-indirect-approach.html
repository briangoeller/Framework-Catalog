Strategy of the Indirect Approach

framework · military science · doctrinal-institutional

Liddell Hart: dislocate opponent psychologically before engaging directly.

The Strategy of the Indirect Approach was substantially articulated by British military theorist B.H. Liddell Hart in his 1929 The Decisive Wars of History (revised editions Strategy 1954 and Strategy: The Indirect Approach 1967), drawing on Liddell Hart's interwar critical analysis of WWI's catastrophic direct-attrition warfare and on substantial historical study of military campaigns. The framework's central commitment is that direct frontal attack against an opponent's strength is rarely the decisive course — successful strategy typically involves disorienting and dislocating the opponent psychologically and physically through indirect approaches (flanking maneuvers, attacks on lines of communication, threats to multiple simultaneous objectives, deception and misdirection) that produce strategic disintegration before direct engagement. Liddell Hart's analysis emphasized: the importance of choosing the line of operation that the opponent does not expect; the value of moving against the opponent's psychological balance rather than physical strength; the principle of presenting the opponent with multiple simultaneous threats that compound strategic dilemmas; the cumulative psychological effects of unexpected moves. The framework substantially influenced 20th-century military thought — German Wehrmacht generals including Heinz Guderian acknowledged Liddell Hart's influence on the development of Blitzkrieg doctrine (though the extent of this influence has been substantially contested by subsequent historians); postwar Israeli military doctrine substantially incorporated indirect-approach thinking; US Marine Corps maneuver warfare doctrine descends partly from this tradition. Critics including J.F.C. Fuller (Liddell Hart's contemporary and intellectual rival) and substantial subsequent historians have argued that Liddell Hart overstated the universality of indirect approach success and selectively interpreted historical evidence to support his thesis. Liddell Hart's broader work on grand strategy and the WWI critique has been similarly contested.

Core components

  • Indirect approach: dislocate opponent psychologically before engaging directly
  • Avoid direct frontal attack against strength
  • Attack lines of communication and supply
  • Present multiple simultaneous threats
  • Operate on line of operation opponent does not expect
  • Psychological balance rather than physical-strength focus
  • Cumulative effects of unexpected moves
  • Connection to maneuver warfare and broader operational art
  • Liddell Hart's influence on German Blitzkrieg (substantially contested by historians)
  • Foundation for postwar Israeli and US military thought

Primary use case

Foundational text in 20th-century military strategy; reference framework in military strategy education globally; basis for substantial military theory literature; integration with maneuver warfare and operational art; influence on Israeli, US Marine Corps, and other militaries' doctrine; foundation for some business strategy applications drawing on military theory; pedagogical reference in strategic studies and history education; influence on grand-strategy literature.

Common criticisms

  • Substantial historical critique: Liddell Hart's claims about indirect approach's universal success have been argued to be selective interpretation of historical evidence — many of his case studies don't fit cleanly into the indirect-approach framework, and counter-examples (where direct approaches succeeded) are underweighted
  • Liddell Hart's broader role in interwar British military thinking has been substantially contested — some historians argue his arguments for limited engagement and avoidance of continental commitment substantively contributed to British appeasement of Nazi Germany
  • his postwar claim of substantial influence on German Blitzkrieg has been contested by historians who note Wehrmacht doctrine developed substantially independently with its own intellectual lineage
  • J.F.C. Fuller's contemporary critique that Liddell Hart oversimplified strategic complexity for popular consumption
  • integration with grand strategy is incomplete — military indirect approach doesn't always serve broader political objectives
  • commercial popularization has produced 'indirect approach' as buzzword applied beyond Liddell Hart's specific framework
  • cross-cultural variation in strategic-cultural receptivity to indirect approaches is substantial
  • the framework's emphasis on physical maneuver may underweight contemporary information and cyber dimensions of strategic competition.
