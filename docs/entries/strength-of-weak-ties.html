Strength of Weak Ties

framework · sociology · structured-empirical

Granovetter's finding that loose acquaintances bridge networks more effectively than close ties.

The Strength of Weak Ties was articulated by Mark Granovetter in his 1973 American Journal of Sociology paper of that name, with the empirical foundation in his Harvard dissertation research on how professional and managerial workers in Newton, Massachusetts found their jobs. Granovetter found, contrary to common assumption, that most successful job-finders had heard of their job through 'weak ties' (acquaintances) rather than 'strong ties' (close friends and family) — and developed the theoretical explanation that close ties tend to share the same network and information, while weak ties bridge across otherwise-disconnected social networks, providing access to non-redundant information. The argument's structural form is general: weak ties are more likely to be 'bridges' between densely-connected clusters, and information flowing through bridges reaches farther than information staying within clusters. Granovetter's 1985 'Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness' substantially extended this work into economic sociology. The strength-of-weak-ties result has been one of the most empirically replicated findings in sociology, with substantial extensions to network theory (Ronald Burt's structural holes, 1992), social capital research (Putnam's bridging vs bonding), and contemporary network science. The result has had substantial practical influence on job-search advice, organizational network analysis, and social-media platform design (LinkedIn explicitly markets weak-tie value).

Core components

  • Strong ties (close friends, family) vs weak ties (acquaintances)
  • Strong ties tend to share networks (redundant information)
  • Weak ties more likely to bridge networks (non-redundant information)
  • Job-search empirical foundation
  • Bridges as structurally important network positions
  • Connection to Burt's structural holes
  • Connection to Putnam's bridging social capital
  • Network-theoretic generalization beyond original domain
  • Substantial empirical replication across contexts

Primary use case

Network analysis and social network theory; job-search advice and labor-market analysis; organizational network analysis (information flow, innovation diffusion); social capital research; foundation for substantial subsequent network-science work; influence on LinkedIn and other professional social-network platform design; basis for many practical recommendations about networking and information seeking.

Common criticisms

  • Original empirical context (white-collar professionals in 1970s Boston) may not generalize to all labor markets — recent research suggests strong ties matter more for some demographics and industries than the original framing suggests
  • the dichotomy between strong and weak ties oversimplifies a continuum, with substantial subsequent work refining
  • gendered and racialized differences in network access mean that weak-tie advantages depend on having access to weak ties in productive networks
  • social-media-era weak ties may differ qualitatively from the 1970s acquaintance ties Granovetter studied
  • some recent research (e.g., Aral and colleagues' large-scale LinkedIn experiment, 2022) suggests strength-of-weak-ties claims need updating for digital networks
  • framework's elegance and citability have produced applications that exceed the empirical evidence
  • the bridging-vs-bonding distinction can be politically loaded when weak ties are presumed always more valuable.
