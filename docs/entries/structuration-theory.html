Structuration Theory

framework · sociology · organizing-schema

Giddens' account of structure and agency as recursively constituted.

Structuration Theory was developed by Anthony Giddens across multiple works in the 1970s and 1980s, with the canonical statement in The Constitution of Society: Outline of the Theory of Structuration (1984). The theory addresses the long-standing structure-agency problem in social theory — whether social phenomena are best explained by structural conditions (functionalism, structuralism, Marxism) or by individual action (interpretive sociology, methodological individualism) — by arguing that structure and agency are mutually constitutive: structure is both the medium and outcome of action. Giddens's 'duality of structure' holds that structures (rules and resources) exist only insofar as they are reproduced through situated action, and action is possible only because actors draw on existing structures. Key concepts include: agency as the capacity to do otherwise; rules and resources as the elements of structure; routine action and the practical consciousness that produces social order; reflexive monitoring of action; time-space distanciation (modern social systems extending across time and space); and the dialectic of control (even subordinates have some power over their superordinates). Structuration theory has had substantial influence in sociology, organization studies (particularly through Wanda Orlikowski's adaptations to information systems and DeSanctis-Poole's adaptive structuration theory), geography, and other social sciences, though it has drawn criticism for being more programmatic than empirically operationalizable.

Core components

  • Duality of structure (structure as medium and outcome of action)
  • Structure as rules and resources
  • Agency as capacity to do otherwise
  • Practical consciousness vs discursive consciousness
  • Routine action and ontological security
  • Reflexive monitoring of action
  • Time-space distanciation
  • Dialectic of control
  • Distinction from structural and interpretivist alternatives
  • Adaptation to organization studies (Orlikowski, DeSanctis-Poole)

Primary use case

Sociological theory bridging structure-agency divide; foundation for substantial work in organizational studies on technology and structuration (Orlikowski's structurational model of technology); reference framework in geography, political science, and information systems; basis for some methodological approaches that aim to capture both structural and agentic dimensions; teaching framework in social-theory education.

Common criticisms

  • Programmatic rather than operational — Giddens articulates how structure and agency are mutually constituted but provides limited methodological guidance for empirical research, and structuration-informed empirical work has been argued to be sociology-as-usual with structuration vocabulary added
  • Margaret Archer's morphogenetic alternative (1995) argues that Giddens conflates structure and agency in ways that lose analytical power, and that they should be analytically separated to study their interaction
  • the theory has been argued to be programmatic synthesis rather than substantive new theory
  • concepts proliferate without clear empirical operationalization
  • the dialectic of control claim about subordinates always having some power has been argued to underweight genuine domination
  • commercial appropriations in organization studies have varied in fidelity to Giddens's framework
  • tension between Giddens's social-theoretic ambitions and his later popular-public-intellectual work on Third Way politics.
