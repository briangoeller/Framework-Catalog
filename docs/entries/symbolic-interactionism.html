Symbolic Interactionism

framework · sociology · organizing-schema

Mead and Blumer's view of society as constituted through meaning-laden interaction.

Symbolic Interactionism is the microsociological tradition treating society as constituted through symbolic communication and meaning-making in everyday interaction, with the self emerging through social interaction rather than preceding it. The tradition's foundational figure is George Herbert Mead, whose Mind, Self, and Society (1934, posthumous, compiled from student notes) articulated the genesis of self through 'taking the role of the other' (the I-Me distinction; the generalized other; the gestural conversation that becomes language), drawing on William James, John Dewey, and Charles Horton Cooley. Herbert Blumer (Mead's student at Chicago) coined the term 'symbolic interactionism' in 1937 and codified the framework's three premises in 1969: humans act toward things based on the meanings they have for them; meanings arise through social interaction; and meanings are modified through interpretive processes. Erving Goffman's dramaturgical extension (separately enriched in this batch) gave symbolic interactionism its most influential contemporary form. The framework dominated 20th-century microsociology through the Chicago School and provided substantial methodological foundation for ethnography, grounded theory, and qualitative research. Distinct from but related to social constructionism (which often draws on symbolic interactionist insights at scale).

Originators

George Herbert Mead (foundational, posthumous Mind Self Society); Herbert Blumer (term coinage, codification); Charles Horton Cooley, William James, John Dewey (precursors); Chicago School tradition high

Year / Decade

Mead's lectures 1900s-1930s; 1934 (Mind, Self, and Society); 1937 (Blumer coins term); 1969 (Blumer Symbolic Interactionism) high

Primary sources

Mead, G.H. (1934, posthumous). Mind, Self, and Society, Blumer, H. (1969). Symbolic Interactionism: Perspective and Method, Cooley, C.H. (1902). Human Nature and the Social Order high

Core components

  • Three premises (Blumer): humans act toward things based on meanings
  • meanings arise through social interaction
  • meanings modified through interpretation
  • Mead's I-Me distinction
  • Generalized other
  • Taking the role of the other
  • Significant symbols
  • Ethnographic and qualitative methods
  • Connection to Chicago School
  • Foundation for grounded theory (Glaser, Strauss)
  • Distinction from macro-structural approaches

Primary use case

Foundational microsociological tradition; methodological foundation for ethnography and grounded theory; analysis of identity formation, role-taking, and everyday social life; foundation for Goffman's dramaturgy and other interactionist extensions; influence on social psychology, particularly the symbolic interactionist tradition within social psychology distinct from cognitive social psychology; reference framework in sociology and communication studies.

Common criticisms

  • Microsociological focus underweights structural and material conditions that shape interactional possibilities
  • meaning-making framing can be politically conservative when it ignores how power shapes which meanings prevail
  • methodological commitment to qualitative fieldwork limits comparison with quantitative traditions
  • coherence across the tradition's various branches (Chicago School, Iowa School, dramaturgical, ethnomethodological-adjacent) is contested
  • risk of voluntarism (treating interaction as fully creative when it operates within substantial constraints)
  • empirical generalization from particular ethnographic studies is challenging
  • tradition's twentieth-century American framing limits cross-cultural application
  • tension with cognitive science accounts of meaning that operate at sub-symbolic levels.
