Theological Virtues

framework · theology · contemplative-tradition

Faith, hope, and love as graces directing the soul to God in Christian tradition.

The Theological Virtues — faith (fides), hope (spes), and charity/love (caritas) — are the three Christian virtues distinguished from the Cardinal Virtues by being supernaturally infused rather than naturally acquired through habituation. The framework's scriptural foundation is Paul's First Letter to the Corinthians 13:13: 'Now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; and the greatest of these is love.' The theological development runs through Augustine's substantial writings on the virtues, Peter Lombard's Sentences (12th century), and Thomas Aquinas's systematic treatment in Summa Theologiae II-II QQ.1-46 (where the Theological Virtues are addressed comprehensively). For Aquinas, the Theological Virtues differ from natural virtues in three ways: their object (God himself, not human flourishing); their cause (divine infusion, not human habituation); and their knowability (revelation, not natural reason). Combined with the four Cardinal Virtues (prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance — separately enriched in Batch 11), the three Theological Virtues constitute the seven virtues of the Christian moral tradition, complementing the seven deadly sins as the structure of Christian moral life. Faith is intellectual assent to God's self-revelation; hope is desire for and confident expectation of eternal beatitude; charity is friendship-with-God that orders all other loves. The framework remains foundational in Catholic moral theology, has substantial presence in Eastern Orthodoxy and Anglicanism, and has been variously received in Reformed traditions that have generally retained the substance while modifying the infusion-and-habit framing.

Originators

Paul of Tarsus (foundational scripture, 1 Corinthians 13:13); Augustine (substantial development); Peter Lombard (12th century systematization); Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae mature treatment) high

Year / Decade

Mid-1st century CE (Paul); 4th-5th century (Augustine); 12th century (Peter Lombard); 13th century (Aquinas) high

Primary sources

Paul. 1 Corinthians 13:13 (mid-1st century CE), Augustine. Enchiridion on Faith, Hope, and Love (~420 CE), Aquinas, T. Summa Theologiae II-II QQ.1-46 (13th century), Pieper, J. (1986). Faith, Hope, Love high

Core components

  • Three virtues: faith (fides), hope (spes), charity/love (caritas)
  • Pauline foundation (1 Corinthians 13:13)
  • Distinction from natural Cardinal Virtues by object (God), cause (divine infusion), and knowability (revelation)
  • Connection to Cardinal Virtues forming the seven Christian virtues
  • Aquinas's systematic Summa treatment
  • Faith as intellectual assent to revelation
  • Hope as desire for and confident expectation of beatitude
  • Charity as friendship with God ordering all loves
  • Pairing with Seven Deadly Sins as structure of Christian moral life

Primary use case

Foundation of Catholic moral theology; substantial presence in Eastern Orthodoxy, Anglicanism, and (with modifications) Reformed Protestantism; reference framework in Christian moral education and spiritual direction; basis for substantial work in philosophical theology on the virtues; integration with Cardinal Virtues in Christian seven-virtues schema; foundation for some contemporary virtue-ethics work that engages theological questions.

Common criticisms

  • Theological virtues' specific framework reflects the Christian theological commitments that ground them (divine grace, supernatural beatitude, revelation) — non-Christian engagement requires either acceptance or substantive translation of those commitments
  • tension between Catholic infusion-and-habit framing and Reformed framings emphasizing imputation and continuing reliance on divine grace
  • Pauline elevation of love as 'the greatest' has produced ongoing theological debate about the relationship among the three virtues
  • Aquinas's account of faith as intellectual assent has been argued by other theological traditions to underweight existential and relational dimensions of faith
  • the distinction between natural and supernatural virtues has been challenged by theologians (Henri de Lubac on 'pure nature' debates) who argue the distinction is not as clean as Thomistic tradition holds
  • cross-cultural application of European Christian framings is uneven
  • tension between virtue-as-disposition and virtue-as-act in actual Christian moral life.
