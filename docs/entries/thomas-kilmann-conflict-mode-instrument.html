Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument

Also known as: TKI

framework · psychology · organizing-schema

Five-mode framework for conflict-handling styles: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating.

The Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI) is a self-report instrument developed by Kenneth W. Thomas and Ralph H. Kilmann in 1974 measuring an individual's preferred conflict-handling style across five modes positioned on two underlying dimensions: assertiveness (concern for one's own outcomes) and cooperativeness (concern for others' outcomes). The five modes are competing (assertive, uncooperative), collaborating (assertive, cooperative), compromising (intermediate on both), avoiding (unassertive, uncooperative), and accommodating (unassertive, cooperative). The framework extends Robert Blake and Jane Mouton's 1964 Managerial Grid (concern-for-people × concern-for-production) to conflict-specific contexts. The instrument uses 30 forced-choice item pairs (ipsative format) yielding scores for each mode. It is among the most widely used assessment instruments in HR, organizational development, leadership training, and dispute-resolution practice, with millions of administrations through Consulting Psychologists Press / The Myers-Briggs Company licensing.

Band notes

Empirical support is moderate; the instrument's psychometric properties are weaker than its widespread organizational use suggests.

Core components

  • Five conflict-handling modes: competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating
  • Two underlying dimensions: assertiveness (concern for self) and cooperativeness (concern for others)
  • 30 forced-choice item pairs (ipsative format)
  • Five scale scores indicating relative preference for each mode
  • Interpretation framework: high vs low scores in each mode have specific organizational interpretations and recommendations
  • Application contexts: leadership training, team development, conflict coaching, mediation training
  • Commercial publishing infrastructure: scoring services, facilitator certification, profile reports
  • Conceptual extension of Blake-Mouton Managerial Grid to conflict-specific behavior

Primary use case

Self-assessment in leadership development, team-building, and conflict-coaching contexts; feedback instrument in 360-degree reviews, executive coaching, and HR development; training input in mediation and dispute-resolution programs; research instrument in organizational behavior and conflict research (with caveat regarding psychometric limitations); facilitator-led workshop content in leadership and team-development programs.

Common criticisms

  • TKI's psychometric properties are weaker than its widespread organizational use suggests — the ipsative (forced-choice) scoring format introduces well-documented psychometric problems including artificial negative correlations among scales, constraints on factor analysis, and difficulty making between-person comparisons (Volkema and Bergmann 1995, Womack 1988, Munduate et al. 1999)
  • test-retest reliability has been reported as moderate (correlations typically 0.6-0.7) which is below standards for high-stakes assessment
  • the five-mode structure has been challenged in factor-analytic studies, with Rahim's ROCI-II (Rahim Organizational Conflict Inventory, 1983) offering a competing framework based on similar conceptual foundation but with normative scoring
  • predictive validity for actual workplace conflict behavior is modest, and self-report measures of conflict style have been shown to correlate weakly with peer-observer ratings
  • cross-cultural validation has produced mixed results, with some studies finding the five-mode structure does not replicate cleanly outside North American samples
  • commercial-publishing context (CPP / Myers-Briggs Company) has produced limited independent peer-reviewed psychometric scrutiny relative to the instrument's commercial scale
  • the framework's apparent normative neutrality across modes (the publisher's framing that 'all modes are appropriate in different situations') may obscure actual differences in long-run effectiveness
  • integration with broader conflict-resolution research (Mary Parker Follett's earlier integrative framework, Fisher and Ury's Getting to Yes principled negotiation) is sometimes argued as theoretically loose.
