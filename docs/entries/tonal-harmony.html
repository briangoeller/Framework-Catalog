Tonal Harmony

Also known as: Common Practice Tonality

framework · music theory · philosophical-tradition

Major-minor harmonic system governing Western art music roughly 1600-1900, organized around tonic-dominant relationships and functional progressions.

Tonal Harmony refers to the major-minor pitch system that governed Western art music from approximately 1600 to 1900, organized around a hierarchical relationship between a tonic key center and surrounding harmonic regions, with progressions structured around tonic-dominant polarity, secondary dominants, sequence patterns, and chromatic voice-leading. The system emerged practically from late-Renaissance modal practice through Corelli, Bach, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, and Wagner, with its theoretical foundation classically attributed to Jean-Philippe Rameau's Traité de l'harmonie (1722) which introduced fundamental-bass theory and established chord-root identification as the basis for harmonic analysis. Tonal Harmony is not a single doctrine but a diffuse tradition encompassing Rameau, Heinichen, Riepel, Kirnberger, and the later 19th-century theoretical consolidation; analytical traditions including Roman numeral analysis, functional harmony (Riemann), Schenkerian analysis, and neo-Riemannian theory all attempt to systematize this practice.

Core components

  • Hierarchical tonic-dominant polarity
  • Triadic harmony as basis (root-position triads, inversions, seventh chords)
  • Cadential progressions (authentic, plagal, deceptive, half)
  • Modulation through pivot chords, common-tone, and chromatic mediant relationships
  • Secondary dominants and applied chords
  • Chromatic voice-leading and chromatic mediant relationships
  • Sequence patterns including descending-fifths, descending-thirds
  • Phrase structure and harmonic rhythm
  • Major and minor mode interaction

Primary use case

Foundational pitch-organizing system underlying Western art music c.1600-1900; basis for the substantial majority of pedagogical music-theory curricula; compositional reference for Western art music, much popular music, jazz, film and game music; historical-stylistic study and historically-informed performance; framework for analytical traditions including Roman numeral, functional, Schenkerian, and neo-Riemannian analysis.

Common criticisms

  • The 'common practice period' construct that Tonal Harmony presupposes has been substantially contested since the 1980s — historical musicologists including Carl Dahlhaus, Reinhard Strohm, and James Webster have argued the tonal system was significantly less unified than the textbook description implies, with Renaissance modal residues, Baroque sequence-driven practice, and late-Romantic chromaticism representing distinct rather than unified systems
  • New Musicology since Susan McClary, Ruth Solie, and Lawrence Kramer has critiqued the tradition's privileging of male European composers and the analytical instruments used to canonize them
  • ethnomusicological work from Bruno Nettl onward documents that the framework's universalist presentation poorly fits non-Western musical traditions and even significant Western popular and folk practice
  • analytical methods built on Tonal Harmony struggle with the late-19th-century chromatic music (Wagner, Liszt, late Brahms) where functional progressions partially break down, motivating neo-Riemannian theory
  • recent corpus-study research (David Temperley, Ian Quinn) has refined some textbook generalizations and challenged others.
