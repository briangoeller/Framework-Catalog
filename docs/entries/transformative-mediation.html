Transformative Mediation

framework · law · organizing-schema

Bush and Folger's mediation framework focused on empowering parties and fostering recognition rather than reaching settlement.

Transformative Mediation is the mediation approach developed by Robert A. Baruch Bush and Joseph P. Folger, articulated in their 1994 book The Promise of Mediation: Responding to Conflict Through Empowerment and Recognition (revised edition 2005). Where conventional mediation typically aims at settlement of substantive disputes, transformative mediation centers two different goals: empowerment (restoring parties' sense of strength, agency, and capacity to make decisions) and recognition (parties' acknowledgment of one another's perspectives, situations, and humanity). The approach views conflict not principally as problem to be solved but as opportunity for moral growth and relational repair. The mediator's role is correspondingly transformed: rather than directing parties toward settlement or evaluating substantive merits, the transformative mediator follows the parties' lead, supports their decision-making, and highlights empowerment and recognition opportunities as they emerge. Settlement may result but is not the primary measure of success — transformation of party interaction is. The Institute for the Study of Conflict Transformation (now Conflict Transformation Center) provides institutional grounding.

Core components

  • Two transformative goals: empowerment (restoration of parties' sense of strength, agency, decision-making capacity) and recognition (parties' acknowledgment of one another's perspectives, situations, and humanity)
  • View of conflict: conflict as opportunity for moral growth and relational repair, not principally as problem to be solved
  • Mediator role: party-led rather than mediator-directed
  • follow parties' lead, support their decision-making, highlight empowerment and recognition opportunities
  • Settlement-agnostic: settlement may result from transformation but is not measure of success
  • Mediator practices: reflecting (capturing what parties say), summarizing (organizing what parties have said), checking-in (offering parties choices about process direction)
  • Avoidance of mediator practices that direct parties: no mediator evaluation of substantive merits, no mediator-proposed solutions, no pressure toward settlement
  • Empowerment shifts: moments where parties recover agency, clarity, calm, decision-making capacity
  • Recognition shifts: moments where parties acknowledge the other's situation, perspective, or humanity
  • Distinction from facilitative mediation: facilitative mediation is settlement-focused with party-led process
  • transformative mediation is transformation-focused with party-led process
  • Distinction from evaluative mediation: evaluative mediation involves mediator opinions on merits and settlement recommendations, fundamentally different from transformative approach
  • Application contexts: family mediation, workplace mediation, community mediation, victim-offender dialogue, ongoing-relationship contexts
  • particular adoption in US Postal Service REDRESS workplace-mediation program (1990s-2000s)

Primary use case

Mediation approach for ongoing-relationship contexts where transformation of party interaction is valuable beyond substantive settlement; applied principally in: workplace mediation (US Postal Service REDRESS program substantial implementation), family mediation, community mediation, victim-offender dialogue (intersecting with restorative-justice tradition), ongoing-relationship business contexts; Institute for the Study of Conflict Transformation (now Conflict Transformation Center): training, certification, scholarly publications; academic and professional reference in mediation, conflict resolution, dispute-resolution, and applied ethics literature; intellectual reference for ongoing-relationship-centered conflict-resolution practice; complementary to Restorative Circles, broader restorative-justice tradition, and humanistic-psychology conflict-resolution approaches; subject of substantial empirical research particularly from US Postal Service REDRESS program implementation.

Common criticisms

  • Transformative Mediation has been substantially debated within the mediation field — Lawrence Susskind (a prominent facilitative-mediation advocate) and others have argued that the approach's settlement-agnostic stance produces under-resolution of disputes that parties came to mediation to resolve, with documented cases of transformative mediations producing transformation-without-settlement that leaves underlying disputes unresolved
  • the approach's intellectual foundation in moral and relational philosophy (care ethics, Buber's I-Thou) has been argued by legal-realist critics to embed particular normative commitments about what mediation should accomplish, with implications about conflict and human relationships that may not match all parties' actual goals
  • the mediator's restraint from substantive intervention can be argued to abandon parties to process dynamics that mediator-engagement could improve, particularly in power-imbalanced contexts where one party's empowerment may not naturally produce the other party's recognition
  • empirical evidence on transformative-mediation effectiveness versus facilitative or evaluative alternatives is mixed — the US Postal Service REDRESS program produced substantial documented benefits but program-context factors (workplace-mediation specific dynamics) limit generalization
  • the approach's substantial training investment (transformative-mediation certification requires extensive coursework and supervised practice) produces practitioner-supply constraints that limit scaling
  • cross-cultural applicability is uneven — the explicit-empowerment and explicit-recognition framework reflects particular Western humanistic-psychology assumptions that may not match cultures with different framings of agency and acknowledgment
  • integration with court-connected mediation programs (where settlement is the institutional goal) produces tension that has limited transformative-mediation adoption in formal-legal contexts
  • the relationship between transformative mediation and restorative justice is conceptually overlapping but practically distinct, with practitioners debating appropriate applications of each.
