Tuckman's Stages

Also known as: Forming-Storming-Norming-Performing

framework · management · organizing-schema

Stage model of small-group development including the later-added 'adjourning.'

Bruce Tuckman's stages of group development, presented in his 1965 Psychological Bulletin review article 'Developmental Sequence in Small Groups', synthesized 50 prior studies of therapy groups, T-groups, natural groups, and laboratory groups into a four-stage sequence: Forming (orientation and dependence), Storming (intragroup conflict), Norming (development of group cohesion), and Performing (functional role-relatedness). Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen added a fifth stage, Adjourning (group dissolution), in their 1977 review. The model has become possibly the most-cited team-development framework in management training despite the original review being a literature synthesis rather than a primary empirical study, with subsequent attempts to rigorously validate the discrete stages producing inconsistent results. Modern team research generally treats the stages as descriptive vocabulary rather than a predictive sequence.

Band notes

Widely cited but limited rigorous empirical validation of discrete stages.

Core components

  • Forming (orientation, dependence on leader)
  • Storming (intragroup conflict, resistance)
  • Norming (cohesion, role acceptance)
  • Performing (functional role-relatedness, task focus)
  • Adjourning (1977 addition: group dissolution and grief)

Primary use case

Team-development vocabulary and training; foundational reference in organizational development and team-coaching practice; pedagogical tool in management education.

Common criticisms

  • Original 1965 paper was a literature synthesis rather than primary empirical work
  • subsequent rigorous tests have not consistently validated the discrete stages or their sequence
  • teams in real organizations often skip stages, return to earlier ones, or operate on multiple stages simultaneously
  • ignores team-composition, task-type, and contextual moderators
  • stage labels can become a self-fulfilling vocabulary that explains away team dynamics rather than analyzing them
  • the rhyming labels make memorability greater than predictive power.
