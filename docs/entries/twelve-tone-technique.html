Twelve-Tone Technique

Also known as: Dodecaphony; Serialism

framework · music theory · philosophical-tradition

Schoenberg's compositional system organizing pitch material around ordered rows containing all twelve pitch classes, manipulated through transposition, inversion, and retrograde.

The Twelve-Tone Technique (also Dodecaphony, originally Method of Composing with Twelve Tones Related Only to One Another) is the compositional system developed by Arnold Schoenberg in the early 1920s organizing pitch material around ordered rows containing all twelve pitch classes, manipulated through transposition, inversion, retrograde, and retrograde-inversion. Schoenberg first publicly articulated the method in his 1923 Suite for Piano Op.25 and described it formally in lectures including the 1941 'Composition with Twelve Tones.' Josef Matthias Hauer developed a parallel and largely independent twelve-tone approach (his Tropen system) through the same period; the priority dispute between Schoenberg and Hauer remains a topic of musicological scholarship. The method's principal early development was by Schoenberg's students Alban Berg (more lyrically tonal-inflected) and Anton Webern (more intervallically rigorous), forming the Second Viennese School. Twelve-tone technique was substantially extended through integral serialism in the 1950s and dominated postwar Western avant-garde composition through the 1960s before declining in influence.

Arnold Schoenberg (foundational, c.1921-1923 articulation); Josef Matthias Hauer (parallel independent development, 1919 onward); Alban Berg and Anton Webern (Second Viennese School development); subsequent extension through Schoenberg's emigration and the postwar European and American serial traditions high

Core components

  • Tone row (Reihe): ordered sequence of all twelve pitch classes (each appearing exactly once)
  • Four basic forms: Prime (P), Inversion (I), Retrograde (R), Retrograde-Inversion (RI)
  • 12 transpositions of each form (P0, P1, ... P11, etc.) producing 48 row forms total
  • Combinatoriality: rows in which segments of two row forms together produce aggregate (all-twelve) collections
  • Hexachordal combinatoriality (Babbitt's substantial elaboration)
  • Row tables and matrix as compositional planning tools
  • Pitch-class operations of transposition, inversion, retrograde implemented as group operations under T_n, I, R
  • Distinction between strict and free twelve-tone composition

Primary use case

Foundational compositional system for the Second Viennese School (Schoenberg, Berg, Webern); framework for postwar serial composition (Babbitt, Boulez, Stockhausen, Nono, Pousseur, Krenek, Dallapiccola); compositional reference for late-20th-century serial and post-serial work; academic reference in 20th-century music history and composition pedagogy; intellectual foundation for integral serialism, pitch-class set theory (retrospectively applied), and broader transformational theory.

Common criticisms

  • Twelve-tone technique has been substantially contested within and outside the tradition — George Rochberg's prominent abandonment of serial composition (chronicled in The Aesthetics of Survival 1984) and the broader 'New Romantic' return to tonality through the 1970s reflected deep dissatisfaction
  • the audience-reception problem (audiences consistently finding twelve-tone music difficult relative to tonal alternatives) was acknowledged even by sympathetic theorists and remains an empirical regularity
  • Carl Dahlhaus, Joseph Auner, and Susan McClary have analyzed the Cold War politicization that elevated serial composition in postwar academic music while marginalizing tonal alternatives
  • Schoenberg's claim that twelve-tone technique would secure 'the supremacy of German music for the next hundred years' has not been vindicated empirically
  • the priority dispute with Josef Matthias Hauer remains historically contested
  • analytical engagement has shifted from Schoenberg's own theoretical claims to pitch-class set theory's retrospective abstraction
  • cross-cultural reception has been limited
  • the Boulez generation's critique that Schoenberg's twelve-tone practice remained tonally-influenced (motivating their own integral-serialism extension) is contested but has been historically influential.
