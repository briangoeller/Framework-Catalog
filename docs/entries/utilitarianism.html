Utilitarianism

framework · philosophy · philosophical-tradition

Maximize aggregate well-being; classical (Bentham, Mill) and contemporary variants.

Utilitarianism is the dominant variant of consequentialism, holding that the right action is the one that produces the greatest aggregate welfare, happiness, or well-being among all affected parties. The classical formulation runs through Jeremy Bentham (An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789), who proposed a hedonistic calculus aggregating pleasures and pains, and John Stuart Mill (Utilitarianism, 1861), who substantially refined the framework by distinguishing higher and lower pleasures and addressing common objections. Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics (1874) provided the most rigorous classical statement. Contemporary utilitarianism encompasses substantial variation: act- vs rule-utilitarianism (does one apply the principle action-by-action or to rule-selection?); hedonistic vs preference vs welfare-list theories of utility; total vs average vs prioritarian aggregation (giving extra weight to the worst-off); two-level utilitarianism (Hare); and global utilitarianism (Pettit). Effective altruism is a contemporary movement applying largely utilitarian reasoning to charitable giving and career choice. Utilitarianism's intuitive force — that suffering and well-being matter, that we should produce more of the latter — has made it the workhorse moral framework for policy analysis and cost-benefit reasoning even where its specific claims are rejected.

Core components

  • Greatest happiness principle
  • Aggregate welfare across all affected parties
  • Hedonistic, preference, or welfare-list theories of utility
  • Act- vs rule-utilitarianism
  • Total vs average aggregation
  • Mill's higher vs lower pleasures distinction
  • Two-level utilitarianism (intuitive everyday level, critical reflective level — Hare)
  • Connection to expected utility theory in decision theory and economics
  • Effective altruism as contemporary applied utilitarianism

Primary use case

Normative ethical theory; foundation for cost-benefit analysis and welfare economics; policy analysis particularly in healthcare resource allocation, environmental policy, and global development; foundation of effective altruism movement; bioethics; applied ethics across domains; intuitive moral reasoning in everyday choices.

Common criticisms

  • Same demandingness, integrity, and aggregation objections as broader consequentialism
  • utility-monster problem: a being who derives enormous utility from any input would have moral priority
  • experience-machine objection (Nozick): if utility is what matters, you should plug into a perpetual-pleasure machine — but most people wouldn't, suggesting other things matter
  • hedonism objection: pleasure isn't the only good
  • rule-utilitarianism either collapses into act-utilitarianism or becomes a form of deontology
  • longtermism (the consequentialist view that the far future overwhelms present concerns) has drawn substantial recent critique
  • effective altruism's 2022 FTX/SBF crisis raised concerns about utilitarian reasoning enabling fraud-justifying calculation
  • Williams's separateness-of-persons objection.
