Wardley Mapping ⚑

framework · management · organizing-schema

Strategy via value-chain components plotted against evolutionary maturity.

Wardley Mapping is a strategy and situational-awareness technique developed by Simon Wardley in the mid-2000s while at Canonical (Ubuntu) and progressively elaborated through his Creative-Commons-licensed online book and conference talks. A Wardley Map plots the components of a value chain — anchored at the top by user need and decomposing downward through capabilities and supporting components — against an evolution axis running through four stages: Genesis (novel, custom-built), Custom-Built, Product (rental), and Commodity (utility). The mapping process exposes which components are strategically defensible (those still in genesis or custom-built) versus which should be outsourced or consumed as commodities, and Wardley pairs the maps with doctrine (universal principles like situational awareness and challenge assumptions) and gameplay (context-specific moves like ecosystem plays, fear-uncertainty-doubt, talent raids).

Core components

  • Value chain anchored on user need
  • Evolution axis (Genesis, Custom-Built, Product, Commodity)
  • Component mapping with positional and movement vectors
  • Doctrine (universal principles)
  • Gameplay (context-specific moves)
  • Climatic patterns (predictable evolutionary forces)

Primary use case

Strategic situational awareness; outsourcing and build-vs-buy decisions; analysis of which capabilities to invest in and which to consume; complement to traditional strategy frameworks; technology-strategy planning.

Common criticisms

  • Component placement on the evolution axis is judgment-heavy and inconsistent across analysts
  • small but passionate practitioner community limits broad empirical validation
  • predictive claims about climatic patterns are difficult to test
  • learning curve is steeper than the apparent simplicity of the diagrams suggests
  • mapping artifacts are easy to produce but hard to use to actually drive decisions
  • less rigorous treatment of competitive dynamics than Porter-style frameworks.
