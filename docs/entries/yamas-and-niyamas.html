Yamas and Niyamas

framework · theology · contemplative-tradition

Yogic ethical restraints (yamas) and observances (niyamas) from Patanjali.

The Yamas and Niyamas are the first two of the eight limbs of Patanjali's yoga, comprising the ethical foundation upon which subsequent meditative practice is built. The five Yamas (restraints, primarily concerning conduct toward others) are: ahimsa (non-violence in thought, word, and deed); satya (truthfulness, not just absence of lying but alignment of speech with reality); asteya (non-stealing, including non-coveting); brahmacharya (sexual restraint, often interpreted as celibacy for monastics and right sexuality for householders); aparigraha (non-grasping, non-greed, non-possessiveness). The five Niyamas (observances, primarily concerning conduct toward oneself) are: shaucha (purity, both physical and mental); santosha (contentment with what is, not in passive resignation but in active acceptance); tapas (austerity, disciplined practice, the heat of transformative effort); svadhyaya (self-study, including study of scripture and reflection on oneself); ishvara-pranidhana (surrender to the divine, devotional orientation). The yamas and niyamas function as preparatory ethical training without which deeper meditative practice cannot bear fruit — the framework reflects the broader Indian tradition's commitment that ethical discipline (sila in Buddhist parallel) is foundational to contemplative attainment. Mahatma Gandhi's interpretation of ahimsa as foundation of nonviolent resistance substantially extended the framework's influence into 20th-century political philosophy.

Originators

Patanjali (Yoga Sutras 2.30-2.45); foundational across yoga tradition; substantial commentary lineage; Mahatma Gandhi (20th-century political extension of ahimsa) high

Year / Decade

Yoga Sutras ~2nd century BCE-4th century CE; ongoing tradition; 20th-century Gandhian extension medium

Primary sources

Patanjali. Yoga Sutras 2.30-2.45, Vyasa. Yoga-Bhasya (commentary), Adele, D. (2009). The Yamas & Niyamas: Exploring Yoga's Ethical Practice, Iyengar, B.K.S. (1993). Light on the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali high

Core components

  • Five Yamas: ahimsa, satya, asteya, brahmacharya, aparigraha
  • Five Niyamas: shaucha, santosha, tapas, svadhyaya, ishvara-pranidhana
  • First two limbs of Patanjali's eight limbs (purvanga)
  • Distinction between yamas (relation to others) and niyamas (relation to self)
  • Foundation for subsequent yogic practice
  • Connection to broader Indian ethical tradition (sila in Buddhism, dharma in Hinduism)
  • Gandhian extension of ahimsa to nonviolent political resistance
  • Householder vs monastic interpretive variation

Primary use case

Ethical foundation of classical Hindu yoga practice; reference framework in yoga teacher training; foundation for Gandhian nonviolence (ahimsa as foundation of satyagraha); integration with broader Hindu ethical thought (dharma); foundation for some contemporary yoga-based ethics curricula; influence on cross-tradition comparative ethics.

Common criticisms

  • Same yoga-modernity tensions as the broader Eight Limbs framework — commercial yoga often nods to yamas and niyamas without sustained engagement
  • specific interpretations vary substantially across teachers, traditions, and household-vs-monastic contexts (brahmacharya as celibacy vs right sexuality being a particularly contested interpretive question)
  • cultural-translation issues in rendering Sanskrit ethical terms into English without distortion (ahimsa as 'non-violence' is partial
  • aparigraha as 'non-greed' captures only part of the meaning)
  • tension between the framework's prescriptive ethics and contemporary moral pluralism
  • risk of fundamentalist readings that take traditional formulations as fixed when commentary tradition shows substantial interpretive flexibility
  • Gandhian appropriation, while substantively important, has been argued to selectively interpret the yamas in ways that depart from traditional contexts.
